Writing did not merely **record** thought: it **transformed** it. By externalising memory, it freed the mind from the burden of retaining everything; by fixing words, it made thought inspectable, comparable, open to criticism. Each major medium that followed — the printing press, the screen, artificial intelligence — has replayed this shift: each time, it is not only the tool that changes, but what thinking means.

This page follows the journey that [*L'Odyssée des idées*](/livres/lodyssee-des-idees/) (in French; 2026) unfolds along its path “Les signes” (“The Signs”): from the first signs to AI, the media first, thought second.

## Before writing: thinking with the voice

For most of its history, humanity thought without writing. An oral culture is not a culture without knowledge — it is a culture in which knowledge has to **fit within living memories**: hence rhythm, formula, verse, repetition. Milman Parry's work on Homer showed it: the epic is not a recited text, it is a mnemonic machine — the poet recomposes at each performance with calibrated formulas. Thinking orally means thinking in flow, in front of witnesses, with no possibility of going back.

## The shift: when memory leaves the body

Writing was born in Mesopotamia some 5,000 years ago — not for poetry, but for **counting**: sheep, jars, debts. This founding detail says the essential: to write, first of all, is to create a binding record — accounting and law before literature.

Then the trace reveals its powers. The anthropologist Jack Goody (*The Domestication of the Savage Mind*; in French, *La Raison graphique*) showed that writing does not merely record pre-existing thoughts: it **creates new intellectual operations**. The list, the table, the column do not exist orally — and it is these that make classification, term-by-term comparison and formal logic possible. Walter Ong (*Orality and Literacy*) summed it up in a formula: writing restructures consciousness. A text, unlike speech, can be reread: one can return to it, confront it with another, find the contradiction in it — **the critical mind is a child of writing**.

The shift had its trial, and it has remained famous: in Plato's *Phaedrus*, King Thamus predicts that writing will produce forgetfulness — people will trust the external sign rather than living memory, and written words, when questioned, “preserve a solemn silence” (275d, tr. Harold N. Fowler). The irony is double: this criticism has reached us only in writing, and Socrates — the philosopher without books, who never wrote a line — still thinks **in us** today only through the texts of his students. Each subsequent medium would replay exactly this trial.

## The printing press: thought changes scale

Gutenberg (around 1450) does not change the nature of the text — he changes its **regime of existence**. Reproduced identically by the thousand, the text becomes standardised: copying errors stop accumulating, editions can be compared, and scholars in different cities can at last discuss **the same text**. The historian Elizabeth Eisenstein showed what modern science owes to this fixity: reproducible tables, maps and figures, on which one person's observation can correct another's.

And print produces something else besides: a **public**. The same text read everywhere at the same time — Luther's Reformation was the first massive demonstration — creates the new entity that would come to be called public opinion. The press, then radio and television, would only accelerate this manufacture.

## Digital media and AI: the third shift

The screen picks up the sequence: unlimited memory, instant access, fluid text that is searched more than it is read. Then artificial intelligence moves the frontier one notch further: after **memory** (writing) and **diffusion** (printing), it is a part of **judgement** that is externalised — formulating, summarising, answering, choosing.

The lesson of the first two shifts holds for the third. Thamus's trial is replayed point for point — AI will produce forgetfulness, laziness, dependence — and it has the same share of truth and the same blind spot: a medium does not abolish thought, it **redistributes** it. The real question is not whether to bless or curse the tool: it is to see what is freed, what atrophies, and who controls the medium. On this last point, the author's anthropic framework extends the analysis: when cognitive externalisation saturates, the burden returns to attention and judgement — this is the [anthropic loop](/en/anthropic-loop/).

## What this history teaches

Three regularities run through the three shifts:

1. **The medium precedes the content** — accounting before literature, almanacs before encyclopaedias, entertainment before online knowledge: the nobler uses come second.
2. **Each externalisation both frees AND atrophies** — writing did weaken the living memory that Thamus defended; it also made Euclid possible. The balance sheet of a medium is never read in a single column.
3. **Each shift has its trial** — and the trial is rarely wrong about the losses, almost always wrong about the balance.

## The complete journey

This page follows a single thread. The book [*L'Odyssée des idées*](/livres/lodyssee-des-idees/) (in French; new 2026 edition, 696 pages) unfolds it among **250 stopovers** — a work, a life, an invention, a shift — linked by seven reading paths, among them “Les signes” (“The Signs”), which runs from the first signs to artificial intelligence. Each stopover follows the same movement: a scene, a shift, a closing note. The one on Socrates — “the philosopher without a book” — ends like this (our translation):

> No definitive answer. No closed doctrine. Nothing but a way of not letting words sleep in peace. […] twenty-four centuries later, he does not teach us what to think. He stops us from believing too quickly that we are thinking.
