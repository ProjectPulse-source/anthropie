Does the academic field displace epistemic disorder toward its margins? This working paper applies the framework of anthropy to the field itself.

## The academic field as a transfer system

A disciplinary field stabilises an epistemic order: its criteria of validity, its publication formats, its hierarchies of legitimacy. This working paper puts forward the hypothesis that in the social sciences, and in economics in particular, the academic field maintains that order by relegating certain anomalies — questions, formats, postures — to its margins, through a mechanism analogous to anthropic transfer.

It tests this on one case, the controversy opened in 2012 by the French Association for Political Economy (AFEP), which requested an "Economics and Society" section of the National Council of Universities: heterodox researchers publish, but in journals that institutional rankings do not value. "A single case does not establish a law," the paper warns, and it distinguishes relegation from legitimate selection: "Not every rejection is relegation; not every marginality is a displaced cost." One speaks of transfer only when three conditions combine: the contribution is ignored without being refuted, no mechanism reassesses it, and its cost falls on identifiable actors.

## Independent research as margin

Independent research occupies a structurally marginal position in the academic economy. It has neither institutional funding, nor seminars and peer review upstream of publication, nor the visibility that affiliations confer. The paper weighs both sides. The outside frees one from certain pressures — calibrated formats, targeted funding, disciplinary socialisation — and allows transversal hypotheses to be explored without having to negotiate a place in a department. It also exposes one to three risks: the absence of institutionalised contradiction, difficult access to data and to networks of cooperation, and theoretical complacency, when a hypothesis "may harden into conviction before it has been sufficiently tested".

The paper names what independent researchers lose in leaving the institution: "not statutory legitimacy, but the forge — that space in which a thesis only stands on condition of having withstood its contradictors".

## Open infrastructures

Do open infrastructures — HAL, ORCID, overlay journals — change this transfer logic? This text argues that they alter its visibility without abolishing its logic.

HAL, the French national open archive, enables a researcher with a listed affiliation, including under the status of independent researcher, to deposit working papers; ORCID provides a persistent identifier independent of affiliation; overlay journals offer peer review built upon open archives. These tools reduce the cost of marginality, but "a deposit on an open archive is not a publication in a peer-reviewed journal", and technical visibility does not mechanically convert into recognition. The paper puts forward two hypotheses about the disorder that openness displaces: information overload, which shifts the work of sorting onto the reader or onto ranking algorithms, and the collapse of quality signals. The transfer changes form — from silent relegation to drowning in noise — not in nature.

## Anthropy applied to itself

This text constitutes a reflexive exercise: applying the framework of anthropy to the field in which it seeks to inscribe itself. It then sets a safeguard: anthropy, a framework developed outside the institution, "cannot serve as a self-sufficient explanation of its own marginality without sinking into circularity". The framework must be tested against observable phenomena — the AFEP controversy, the Matthew effect, information overload — independently of its author's position; if the hypothesis fails that test, it will have to be revised or abandoned.
