Who can get a law passed in France?
Do laws mostly come from the Government?
Since the 15th legislature (2017-2022; a legislature is the term of the National Assembly), most ordinary laws promulgated in France, excluding treaties, have arisen from a bill tabled by a deputy or a senator; for the 15th, narrowly (at least 103 laws out of 202).
This result depends on what is counted: adding the laws with a procedure of their own (the State and social security budgets, laws that supplement or amend the Constitution), the parliamentary majority only appears with the 16th (2022-2024).
Yet about nine in ten private members' bills tabled in the National Assembly were not debated in plenary before their legislature ended.
The two findings fit together because getting a law passed takes several keys, which the Constitution shares out: the Prime Minister and members of Parliament can table bills, but the Government has priorities on the agenda and alone can ask the National Assembly to reach a final decision.
The passing of statutes belongs to Parliament, and three situations must be distinguished: the passing of a bill at one stage without the National Assembly voting on it (article 49, paragraph 3), the bringing into force of budget provisions by ordinance without a statute passed by vote (articles 47 and 47-1), and the passing of a law by referendum (article 11).

The Constitution gives Parliament the passing of statutes: “Parliament shall pass statutes.” (art.
24).
Three situations stand apart from it.
When the Prime Minister makes a bill an issue of a vote of confidence, the bill is considered passed at that stage, unless a motion of no confidence is carried (art.
49, para.
3).
If Parliament has not reached a decision within the time limits, the provisions of a Finance Bill or Social Security Financing Bill (the State budget or the social security budget) may be brought into force by ordinance, without becoming a statute passed by vote (arts.
47 and 47-1).
Lastly, a law may be passed by referendum (art.
11).
This page follows the route almost every law takes, through Parliament: who tabled, law by law, the laws promulgated in France since 2012, what became of the private members’ bills tabled in the National Assembly, how many times article 49, paragraph 3 was used.
The Constitution is quoted in the English translation published by the Conseil constitutionnel.
Short answer.
Getting a law passed takes four keys.
Tabling: “Both the Prime Minister and Members of Parliament shall have the right to initiate legislation.” (art.
39).
Reaching the floor: each House sets its agenda, but the Government has priorities in it (art.
48).
Concluding: the two Houses must agree on an identical text or, failing that, the National Assembly has the final say, which only the Government can ask it to exercise (art.
45).
And the Prime Minister can make the passing of a bill an issue of a vote of confidence, at the risk of a no-confidence vote (arts.
49 and 50).
So the Government does not get laws passed on its own, and members of Parliament, who have tabled most of the laws promulgated since 2017 in the scope studied, do not hold the other keys on their own.
Who initiated the laws promulgated?
Do laws mostly come from the Government?
The origin of each law promulgated, at the tabling of the text, makes it possible to check.
A text from the Government is a government bill; one from a deputy or a senator, a private member’s bill.
The count covers ordinary laws, those that follow the common procedure.
It leaves aside the laws with a procedure or an origin of their own: laws authorising a treaty (which allow France to ratify or approve an international agreement), finance and social security financing acts (the State and social security budgets), institutional acts (provided for by the Constitution to specify some of its rules) and constitutional acts (which amend the Constitution).
Laws promulgated, assigned to the legislature of their promulgation, by the origin of the text at its initial tabling.
Scope studied: ordinary laws, excluding laws authorising a treaty, finance and social security financing acts, institutional acts and constitutional acts.

The finding in one sentence.
In the scope studied, the share of the laws promulgated that arose from a private member’s bill is 46% during the 14th legislature (2012-2017), then above half: 53% during the 15th (at least 51%), 66% during the 16th and 80% during the 17th, ongoing on 6 October 2026.
What origin does not tell.
It says who tabled the text, not who wanted it nor what became of it.
A private member’s bill may have been prepared with a ministry or supported by the Government; a government bill may be deeply changed by Parliament.
The rising share of private members’ bills therefore does not measure growing autonomy of members of Parliament: none of these series observes such influences.
Does the scope change the answer?
For the 15th legislature, yes.
During the 15th, 53% of the laws in the scope studied are of parliamentary origin, but only 46% counting all kinds except treaties (114 laws out of 249).
This second count puts finance, institutional and constitutional acts back in; it also gives 42% during the 14th, 58% during the 16th and 76% during the 17th.
The parliamentary majority of the 15th therefore depends on the scope chosen.
That of the 16th and 17th does not.
These laws are left out of the main count because their origin or procedure is set apart.
Articles 47 and 47-1 of the Constitution speak only of Finance Bills and Social Security Financing Bills, and all these acts come here from a government bill.
Institutional acts follow their own rules (art.
46): failing agreement between the two Houses, the National Assembly may pass them on a final reading only by an absolute majority of its members, and those relating to the Senate must be passed in identical terms by the two Houses.
Laws authorising a treaty, all from a government bill, are counted nowhere.
The wording of article 46
Article 46: “failing agreement between the two Houses, the text may be passed by the National Assembly on a final reading only by an absolute majority of the Members thereof.” And: “Institutional Acts relating to the Senate must be passed in identical terms by the two Houses.”

What was checked, and how
Each law promulgated since June 2012 is matched, by its number in the Journal officiel, between the National Assembly’s open data and the Senate’s Dosleg database.
When both sources know a law, they give the same origin for all laws.
Some laws of the 14th and 15th legislatures are missing from the Assembly’s files, because their file was opened under the previous legislature: their origin rests on the Senate alone, hence the “at least” bound in the table and the triangle in the figure.

How many private members’ bills are debated in plenary?
Few.
In each of the three completed legislatures, about nine in ten ordinary private members’ bills tabled first in the National Assembly had not been debated in plenary by the end of the legislature, according to the Assembly’s data: 1,402 out of 1,555 during the 14th (90%), 1,964 out of 2,160 during the 15th (91%), 1,131 out of 1,241 during the 16th (91%).
The ongoing 17th is not counted.
A bill that was not debated in plenary has been neither rejected nor barred, and its content has not necessarily disappeared: it may be taken up in another text, or in an amendment.
Nor do these fractions compare from one legislature to the next like rates: the 16th, cut short by the dissolution, left texts less than half as much time to be considered (median time from tabling to the end of the legislature: 381 days, against 1,064 during the 14th).
The worst case: bills the Assembly's bulletins do not count
These figures count the bills tabled in the Assembly’s open data.
Its statistical bulletins count slightly fewer: 17, 5 and 3 fewer private members’ bills depending on the legislature, with no list that would identify the texts concerned.
Removing this whole excess both from the total and from the bills not debated leaves 1,385 out of 1,538, 1,959 out of 2,155 and 1,128 out of 1,238: still about nine in ten.

How do the two findings fit together?
They do not contradict each other, because they do not bear on the same texts.
The first starts from the laws promulgated, Senate bills included, assigned to the legislature of their promulgation; the second from the private members’ bills tabled in the Assembly, assigned to the legislature of their tabling.
A small share of private members’ bills that succeed can supply most of the laws promulgated, because private members’ bills far outnumber government bills: according to the Assembly’s bulletins, 1,694 private members’ bills were tabled there on first reading during the 14th legislature, against 294 government bills, and the gap exceeds a factor of five in each of the three completed legislatures.

Who decides what reaches the floor?
Each House, first: under article 48, “the agenda shall be determined by each House.” The same article reserves two sitting weeks out of four for the Government, for the texts and debates it asks to have placed on the agenda, in the order it sets.
It also gives it priority, at its request, for certain texts, including the State and social security budget bills.
And it guarantees one sitting day a month to opposition and minority groups.
The agenda is therefore shared, and priority on the agenda does not guarantee that a text will be passed.
Another rule limits what members of Parliament can propose: their bills and amendments are not admissible where their enactment would reduce public revenue, or create or increase public expenditure (art.
40).
The National Assembly allows no offset for expenditure, but allows a loss of revenue offset by another revenue (the “gage”).
The data on this page do not say which of these filters set aside a given bill: they contain neither the decisions to place bills on the agenda nor the rulings of inadmissibility.
The wording of articles 48 and 40
Article 48, paragraph 2: “During two weeks of sittings out of four, priority shall be given, in the order determined by the Government, to the consideration of texts and to debates which it requests to be included on the agenda.” Paragraph 3: “In addition, the consideration of Finance Bills, Social Security Financing Bills and, subject to the provisions of the following paragraph, texts transmitted by the other House at least six weeks previously, as well as bills concerning a state of emergency and requests for authorization referred to in article 35, shall, upon Government request, be included on the agenda with priority.” Paragraph 5: “One day of sitting per month shall be given to an agenda determined by each House upon the initiative of the opposition groups in the relevant House, as well as upon that of the minority groups.”
Article 40: “Private Members' Bills and amendments introduced by Members of Parliament shall not be admissible where their enactment would result in either a diminution of public revenue or the creation or increase of any public expenditure.”
According to the National Assembly’s summary sheet no.
52 (September 2023), on “public revenue”, in the plural: « L'emploi du pluriel a pour effet d'autoriser la compensation d'une perte de recettes par l'augmentation d'une autre recette. » [our translation: “The use of the plural has the effect of allowing a loss of revenue to be offset by an increase in another revenue.”].
On “public expenditure”, in the singular (“une charge publique” in the French text): « la création ou l'aggravation d'une charge ne peut être gagée » [our translation: “the creation or increase of a charge cannot be offset”].
Who has the final say between the two Houses?
The principle is agreement: “Every Government or Private Member's Bill shall be considered successively in the two Houses of Parliament with a view to the passing of an identical text.” (Constitution, art.
45).
When agreement does not come, article 45 sets a sequence, and each step has its actor.
A joint committee, with equal numbers of deputies and senators, may be convened to propose a text on the provisions still under debate.
It may be convened after two readings in each House, or after a single one if the Government has applied the accelerated procedure without the Conferences of Presidents of the two Houses being jointly opposed.
Convening it falls to the Prime Minister or, for a private member’s bill, to the Presidents of the two Houses acting jointly.
The committee’s text goes through the Government: it is the Government that may submit it to both Houses, and no amendment to it is admissible without its consent.
If that fails, the final say of the National Assembly can only come from the Government: if the committee fails to agree on a common text, or if that text is not passed, the Government may, after a further reading in each House, ask the National Assembly to reach a final decision.
This holds for a private member’s bill too.
The Government can open this procedure; it cannot guarantee that the Assembly will pass the text.
Lastly, the Government has a floor tool, the single vote (art.
44): at its request, the House proceeds to a single vote on all or part of the text, on the sole basis of the amendments the Government has proposed or accepted.
The House still votes, but on the text as the Government accepts it.
The wording of articles 45 and 44
Article 45, paragraph 2: “If, as a result of a failure to agree by the two Houses, it has proved impossible to pass a Government or Private Member's Bill after two readings by each House or, if the Government has decided to apply the accelerated procedure without the two Conferences of Presidents being jointly opposed, after a single reading of such Bill by each House, the Prime Minister, or in the case of a Private Members' Bill, the Presidents of the two Houses acting jointly, may convene a joint committee, composed of an equal number of members from each House, to propose a text on the provisions still under debate.” Paragraph 3: “The text drafted by the joint committee may be submitted by the Government to both Houses for approval.
No amendment shall be admissible without the consent of the Government.” Paragraph 4: “If the joint committee fails to agree on a common text, or if the text is not passed as provided in the foregoing paragraph, the Government may, after a further reading by the National Assembly and by the Senate, ask the National Assembly to reach a final decision.”
Article 44, paragraph 3: “If the Government so requests, the House before which the Bill is tabled shall proceed to a single vote on all or part of the text under debate, on the sole basis of the amendments proposed or accepted by the Government.”
What does article 49, paragraph 3 change?
It allows the Prime Minister, after deliberation by the Council of Ministers, to make the passing of a bill an issue of a vote of confidence before the National Assembly: the bill is then considered passed at that stage of its consideration, without the deputies voting on it, unless a motion of no confidence, tabled within the following twenty-four hours, is carried.
The paragraph covers Finance Bills and Social Security Financing Bills; beyond those texts, the Prime Minister may use the procedure for only one other government or private member’s bill per session.
The bill is not thereby finally passed: the use covers one stage, and consideration goes on.
The same bill may be subject to several uses, including during the same reading; each is counted once.
The motion of no confidence targets the Government, and paragraph 2 of the article allows it to be carried only by a majority of the members of the Assembly, only the votes cast in favour being counted.
If it is carried, the bill is not considered passed at that stage, and the Government must tender its resignation (art.
50).
The wording of articles 49 and 50
Article 49, paragraph 3: “The Prime Minister may, after deliberation by the Council of Ministers, make the passing of a Finance Bill or Social Security Financing Bill an issue of a vote of confidence before the National Assembly.
In that event, the Bill shall be considered passed unless a resolution of no-confidence, tabled within the subsequent twenty-four hours, is carried as provided for in the foregoing paragraph.” And: “In addition, the Prime Minister may use the said procedure for one other Government or Private Members' Bill per session.” Paragraph 2: “Solely votes cast in favour of the no-confidence resolution shall be counted and the latter shall not be passed unless it secures a majority of the Members of the House.”
Article 50: “When the National Assembly passes a resolution of no-confidence, or when it fails to endorse the Government programme or general policy statement, the Prime Minister shall tender the resignation of the Government to the President of the Republic.”
Uses of article 49, paragraph 3, each counted once, by the categories of that paragraph: the public finance programming act falls under “one other bill”.
This count covers all bills, finance bills included.

Counting laws rather than uses changes the picture.
During the 16th legislature, 5 of the 8 finance or social security financing acts promulgated went through at least one use, at one stage or more of their consideration; the others went through none.
During the ongoing 17th, it is 3 out of 6.
These numbers describe the procedure followed, not the content of the bills nor what a vote would have produced, and they do not explain why use differs from one legislature to another.
No motion of no confidence was carried on these uses, from the 14th to the 16th legislature.
On 2 December 2024, article 49, paragraph 3 was used on the joint committee text of the Social Security Financing Bill for 2025; according to the same sheet, « La première motion déposée a été adoptée selon la majorité qualifiée requise » [our translation: “The first motion tabled was carried by the required qualified majority”].
The bill was not considered passed at that stage, and the Government had to resign.
The two other situations.
If Parliament has not reached a decision within the time limit set by the Constitution, the provisions of the State budget bill (art.
47) or of the social security budget bill (art.
47-1) may be brought into force by ordinance.
These ordinances do not make a statute passed by vote: they bring the provisions of a bill into force.
A referendum, by contrast, passes a law without Parliament voting on its text: where the outcome is favourable to the bill, the President of the Republic promulgates the resulting statute (art.
11).
When it is held on the Government’s recommendation, Parliament debates without voting: the Government makes a statement before each House, followed by a debate.
The page Can the French president call a referendum? counts them.
The wording of articles 47, 47-1 and 11
Article 47: “Should Parliament fail to reach a decision within seventy days, the provisions of the Bill may be brought into force by Ordinance.” Article 47-1: “Should Parliament fail to reach a decision within fifty days, the provisions of the Bill may be implemented by Ordinance.”
Article 11: “Where the outcome of the referendum is favourable to the Government Bill or to the Private Members' Bill, the President of the Republic shall promulgate the resulting statute within fifteen days following the proclamation of the results of the vote.” And: “Where the referendum is held on the recommendation of the Government, the latter shall make a statement before each House and the same shall be followed by a debate.”
What was checked, and how
The uses are read in the National Assembly’s legislative files, and counted twice by two different routes of the same producer, with the same dates.
They are compared, session by session, with the Assembly’s statistical bulletins: same numbers of uses and of motions.
A single date differs, by one day, for a use in November 2022; the official record of the sitting confirms the date in the data.
For a few motions, the data do not contain the vote; the bulletins give their ballots, all below the required majority.
The finance acts concerned are found through the Assembly’s file identifier, common to both files; the script checks that each use classed as on a Finance Bill or Social Security Financing Bill bears on an act of that kind, and no other.
How to judge a promise that requires a law?
Three questions are enough to place a promise of a law, without assuming anything about its content.
Who will table the text?
The Government or a member of Parliament.
A bill or an amendment from a member of Parliament cannot create or increase public expenditure, and a loss of revenue must be offset by another revenue (art.
40, as the National Assembly applies it); this rule does not cover the Government’s texts.
Who will put it on the agenda?
The Government in its priority weeks, a House in its own, an opposition or minority group on its monthly day (art.
48).
Who will have the final say?
Agreement between the two Houses; failing that, the National Assembly, if the Government asks it to (art.
45); or a use of article 49.3, at the risk of a no-confidence vote (arts.
49 and 50).
A law passed is not yet a law applied: the page Does a law apply as soon as it is passed? takes it from there.
And the page What can the French president decide alone? deals with what he decides without going through a law.
What this page does not say
It does not measure the Government’s influence on private members’ bills, nor Parliament’s on government bills: the origin of a law is not its content, and amendments are not counted.
It does not link the number of uses of article 49.3 to the composition of the Assembly: a coincidence in time does not establish a link.
It does not say what becomes of the bills that are not debated, nor why they did not reach the floor.
The counts of bills tabled do not match the Assembly’s bulletins text by text: the sentence on private members’ bills rests only on the worst-case bound.
It counts neither budget ordinances nor laws passed by referendum.

Frequently asked questions
Who can propose a law in France?
Under article 39 of the Constitution, "Both the Prime Minister and Members of Parliament shall have the right to initiate legislation." (English translation published by the Conseil constitutionnel).
A text from the Government is a government bill (projet de loi); one from a deputy or a senator is a private member's bill (proposition de loi).
In the scope studied (excluding laws authorising a treaty, finance and social security financing acts, institutional acts and constitutional acts), 55 of the 83 laws promulgated during the 16th legislature arose from a private member's bill, 28 from a government bill.
A private member's bill is, however, not admissible if it creates or increases public expenditure, nor if it reduces public revenue without offsetting the loss by another revenue (article 40, as the National Assembly applies it).
Can a law be passed in France without Parliament voting on its text?
Three situations must be distinguished: making the passing of a bill an issue of a vote of confidence, which has the bill considered passed at one stage without the deputies voting on it, unless a motion of no confidence is carried (articles 49, paragraph 3, and 50); bringing a budget into force by ordinance if Parliament has not reached a decision within the time limits, without a statute passed by vote (articles 47 and 47-1); passing a law by referendum (article 11).

The figures, quotations, checks and charts are produced by a single script.
It reads the origin of laws, the private members’ bills and the uses of article 49.3 in the data of the National Assembly, the Senate and the Journal officiel, and finds each quotation word for word in its archived document.
The bills counted by the Assembly’s statistical bulletins, published as images, are transcribed by hand and serve only for the worst case; the bulletins’ uses of article 49.3, read in their text, serve as a cross-check.
The script stops if a figure no longer meets the conditions on which the main sentences rest.

In the scope studied (excluding laws authorising a treaty, finance and social security financing acts, institutional acts and constitutional acts), the share of the laws promulgated in France that arose from a private member’s bill is 46% during the 14th legislature, 53% during the 15th (at least 51%), 66% during the 16th and 80% during the ongoing 17th; counting all kinds except treaties, the 15th falls below half (46%).
About nine in ten ordinary private members’ bills tabled in the National Assembly were not debated in plenary before their legislature ended, from the 14th to the 16th, worst case included.
Article 49, paragraph 3 was used 23 times during the 16th legislature, 21 of them on Finance Bills or Social Security Financing Bills.
