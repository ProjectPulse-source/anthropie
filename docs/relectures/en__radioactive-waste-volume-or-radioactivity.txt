Radioactive waste: does volume tell the problem?
Radioactive waste is first counted in cubic metres: 1.89 million at end-2024.
But volume says neither where the radioactivity is nor what remains unresolved.
At end-2021, high-level waste made up 0.25% of the volume and 97.1% of the declared radioactivity; its final disposal is planned at Bure (Cigéo), but not yet licensed.

Where is the radioactivity?
At end-2021, the latest year in which Andra’s inventory declares the radioactivity of each category, France held 1.76 million cubic metres of radioactive waste in the five categories of its balances, in conditioned equivalent.
Over nine tenths of this volume (91.7%) was low- and intermediate-level short-lived waste and very-low-level waste: it carried 0.12% of the declared radioactivity.
High-level waste, for its part, made up 0.25% of the volume, a few thousandths, and 97.1% of the radioactivity; together with long-lived intermediate-level waste, 99.8%.
The categories that hold most of the declared radioactivity are therefore not those that take up the most volume, and they do not fall under the same management routes.
Their radioactivity does not, on its own, measure their dangerousness or the risk to which the population is exposed.
Volume in conditioned equivalent and declared radioactivity, in becquerels, for the same year; disposal routes according to Andra (National Inventory, open data; Les Essentiels 2026).
The result in one sentence.
In France, at end-2021, high-level waste made up 0.25% of the volume of radioactive waste and 97.1% of its declared radioactivity; its final disposal, planned at Bure (Cigéo), is not yet licensed.
Volume has kept growing since: 1.89 million cubic metres at end-2024, including 4,717 m³ of high-level waste, still 0.25% of the total.
Radioactivity is declared only at the inventory’s editions.
What the chart shows, and what it does not
Two ways of measuring the problem circulate.
The first counts cubic metres: the more waste, the bigger the problem.
The second looks at where the radioactivity is and what remains unresolved.
The chart shows that the two measures give very different hierarchies: by volume, short-lived and very-low-level waste dominates; by radioactivity, high-level waste.
It also shows that the three long-lived categories (high-level, intermediate- and low-level long-lived) have no final disposal route in operation yet, including low-level long-lived waste, which is barely radioactive.
This reflects management constraints linked in particular to half-life, activity level and the properties of the waste; it does not allow the situation to be attributed to lifespan alone.
The inventory declares radioactivity at its editions: five files record it (2013, 2014, 2015, 2016 and 2021).
In each, high-level waste accounts for between 84.7 and 97.1% of the recorded radioactivity, and high-level together with long-lived intermediate-level waste for at least 99.7%.
The available data do not, without further harmonisation, explain the variation from one edition to the next.
Part of the volume has no declared radioactivity; assuming it has the average activity of its category, an unverified assumption, the share of high-level waste would be 96.7%.
What the chart does not show.
The becquerel measures a number of decays per second.
It does not directly measure the dose received, the toxicity of a radionuclide, health risk, or the difficulty or cost of management.
The chart shows how declared radioactivity is distributed across categories; it ranks neither risks nor costs.
Where is this waste today?
At end-2024, 91% of the volume of low- and intermediate-level short-lived waste is recorded in Andra’s disposal facilities: in the Aube, in operation, and in the Manche, closed to new deliveries since 1994 and under surveillance.
68% of very-low-level waste is at Cires, in the Aube.
The categories that concentrate the radioactivity have no final disposal yet.
According to Andra, high-level and long-lived intermediate-level waste is “produced and stored on its production sites, pending the creation of the disposal facility intended to receive it: Cigéo” (our translation); disposal for low-level long-lived waste is under study.
Cigéo is a deep geological disposal facility planned at Bure (Meuse).
Andra applied for its creation licence in January 2023.
The public inquiry closed on 16 July 2026, and the inquiry commission issued a favourable opinion in August, with two reservations and two recommendations.
An inquiry opinion is not a licence: as of mid-September 2026, the decision on the creation licence was still to be taken.
What remains outside the balances
Some waste does not appear in Andra’s balances.
The main set is the historical uranium conversion residues at Orano’s Malvési site: Andra reports about 690,000 m³ (ECRIN facility, evaporation and settling ponds), states that these volumes “are not counted in the balances” and that “the search for a safe long-term management route on the Malvési site is under way” (our translations).
Historical disposals, which fall to their holders, come on top.
What these data do not say
Radioactivity is neither risk nor cost.
It measures a number of decays per second; danger depends on the radionuclides, their half-life and exposure.
One vintage for the comparison.
Volume and radioactivity are compared at end-2021, the latest edition declaring radioactivity; end-2024 volumes are given separately, without radioactivity.
Volumes without declared radioactivity.
The variant crediting them with the average activity of their category rests on an assumption: it tests the sensitivity of the result, it does not validate it.
An unreconciled outside-balance scope.
Since the end-2021 file, the open file has classified outside the categories of the balances a volume that reaches 1.33 million cubic metres at end-2024; its “conversion residues” family (0.89 million m³) does not match the volumes Andra publishes for Malvési.
That total is not interpreted here.
Waste, not materials.
Radioactive materials (depleted uranium, spent fuel awaiting reprocessing) are not waste under French law and are not included in these figures.
The status of disposal routes and of Cigéo is taken from published sources.
It is not computed by the script and is checked at each step of the procedure.
