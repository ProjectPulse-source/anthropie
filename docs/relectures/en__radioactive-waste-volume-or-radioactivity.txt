Radioactive waste: does volume tell the problem?
Radioactive waste is first counted in cubic metres: 1.89 million at end-2024 in Andra’s balances.
But volume says neither where the radioactivity is nor what remains unresolved.
At end-2021, high-level waste made up 0.25% of the volume and 97.2% of the declared radioactivity (mostly an upper bound, not an exact measurement); its final disposal is planned at Bure (Cigéo), but not yet licensed.

Where is the radioactivity?
At end-2021, the latest year for which Andra’s inventory declares the radioactivity of each category, France held 1.76 million cubic metres of radioactive waste, in conditioned equivalent, in the five categories of Andra’s balances.
Over nine tenths of this volume (91.7%) was low- and intermediate-level short-lived waste and very-low-level waste: it carried 0.12% of the declared radioactivity.
High-level waste, for its part, made up 0.25% of the volume — a few thousandths — and 97.2% of the declared radioactivity (mostly an upper bound, not an exact measurement); together with long-lived intermediate-level waste, it held 99.9% of the declared radioactivity.
The categories that hold most of the declared radioactivity are therefore not those that take up the most volume, and they do not fall under the same management routes.
Their radioactivity does not, on its own, measure how hazardous they are or the risk to which the public is exposed.

The result in one sentence.
In France, at end-2021, high-level waste made up 0.25% of the volume of radioactive waste recorded in Andra’s balances and 97.2% of the declared radioactivity (mostly an upper bound, not an exact measurement); its final disposal, planned at Bure (Cigéo), is not yet licensed.
Volume has kept growing since: 1.89 million cubic metres at end-2024, including 4,717 m³ of high-level waste, still 0.25% of the total.
Radioactivity, by contrast, is declared for the bulk of the volume only in the files of the inventory’s editions, the latest at end-2021.
What the chart shows, and what it does not
The two rankings are very different, and the chart sets them side by side: by volume, short-lived and very-low-level waste dominates; by declared radioactivity, high-level waste.
But neither, on its own, measures the doses to which a population may be exposed, how long the waste remains hazardous or the cost of management solutions.
The chart also shows that high-level waste and the two long-lived categories (intermediate- and low-level long-lived) have no final disposal route in operation yet, including low-level long-lived waste, which holds only 0.01% of the declared radioactivity.
This reflects management constraints linked in particular to half-life, activity level and the properties of the waste; it does not allow the situation to be attributed to lifespan alone.
The radioactivity of each category appears in five files (2013, 2014, 2015, 2016 and 2021): declared every year up to the end-2016 data, it has since been declared, for the bulk of the volume, only in the inventory’s editions; the files for other years give it for less than a thousandth of the volume.
In each of these five files, high-level waste accounts for between 84.7% and 97.2% of the radioactivity recorded in the five categories of the balances, and high-level together with long-lived intermediate-level waste for at least 99.8%.
The available data do not, without further harmonisation, allow these shares to be read as a trend, nor the variation from one edition to the next to be explained.
Part of the volume has no declared radioactivity; assuming it has the average activity of its category, an unverified assumption, the share of high-level waste would be 96.8%.
What the chart does not show.
The becquerel measures a number of decays per second.
It does not directly measure the dose received, the toxicity of a radionuclide, health risk, or the difficulty or cost of management.
The chart shows how declared radioactivity is distributed across categories; it ranks neither risks nor costs.
Where is this waste today?
At end-2024, 91% of the volume of low- and intermediate-level short-lived waste is recorded in Andra’s disposal facilities: the one in the Aube, which is in operation, and the one in the Manche, which has received no waste since 1994 and is in its closure phase.
Cires, in the Aube, holds 68% of very-low-level waste.
The categories in which the radioactivity is concentrated have no final disposal yet.
According to Andra, some high-level and long-lived intermediate-level waste is “produced and stored on its production sites, pending the creation of the disposal facility intended to receive it: Cigéo” (our translation); disposal for low-level long-lived waste is under study.
Cigéo is a deep geological disposal facility planned at Bure (Meuse).
The creation licence procedure for Cigéo
Step 1 January 2023: Andra applies for Cigéo’s creation licence
Step 2 16 July 2026: the public inquiry closes
Step 3 August 2026: the inquiry commission issues a favourable opinion, with two reservations and two recommendations; this opinion is not a licence
Then The procedure provides for a preliminary draft decree sent to Andra for comments, an opinion from the Nuclear Safety and Radiation Protection Authority (ASNR), and a decree issued after consulting the Conseil d’État
As of 12 November 2026, no creation licence decree had been published in the Journal officiel.
What remains outside the balances
Some waste does not appear in Andra’s balances.
This includes the historical uranium conversion residues at Orano’s Malvési site: Andra reports about 690,000 m³ (ECRIN facility, evaporation and settling ponds) and states that these volumes “are not counted in the balances” and that “the search for a safe long-term management route on the Malvési site is under way” (our translations).
Also outside the balances are, in particular, residues from the processing of uranium ore at former mining sites and waste with enhanced natural radioactivity: Andra counts them in tonnes (tens of millions), so they cannot be added to these cubic metres.
Historical disposal sites, which are the responsibility of their holders, are also absent from them.
What these data do not say
Radioactivity is neither risk nor cost.
It is a number of decays per second; danger depends on the radionuclides, their half-lives and exposure.
Declared radioactivity, mostly an upper bound.
In the end-2021 file, 88.4% of the declared radioactivity of the five categories (89.7% for high-level waste) carries the “<” sign, which, according to the inventory’s data dictionary, flags an upper-bound activity: a maximum, not an exact measurement.
The page’s shares are shares of these declared values; the data file gives, by category and year, the declared activity and its upper-bound part.
One vintage for the comparison.
Volume and radioactivity are compared at end-2021, the latest year for which the inventory declares the radioactivity of each category; end-2024 volumes are given separately, without radioactivity.
Volumes without declared radioactivity.
The variant crediting them with the average activity of their category rests on an assumption: it tests the sensitivity of the result; it does not validate it.
An unreconciled scope outside the balances.
Since the end-2021 file, the open file has classified, outside the categories of the balances, a volume that reaches 1.33 million cubic metres at end-2024; its uranium conversion residues family (0.89 million m³) includes, in addition to the volumes Andra publishes for Malvési, an entry for waste rock from a former sulphur mine that Andra does not count in those volumes.
That total is not interpreted here.
Waste, not materials.
Radioactive materials (depleted uranium, spent fuel awaiting reprocessing) are not waste under French law, unless the administrative authority reclassifies them as such (Environment Code, Articles L.
542-1-1 and L.
542-13-2), and are not included in these figures.

The figures on this page describe such a legacy; they do not demonstrate that reading.

Frequently asked questions
What share of the volume of radioactive waste is high-level waste?
At end-2021, the latest year for which the inventory declares the radioactivity of each category, high-level waste accounted for 0.25% of the volume of the five categories in Andra’s balances and 97.2% of the declared radioactivity (mostly an upper bound, not an exact measurement).
At end-2024, it takes up 4,717 m³ in conditioned equivalent, 0.25% of the volume.
Is the most radioactive waste disposed of?
No: it is held in storage — that is, placed temporarily with the intention of retrieving it — and not disposed of, a term French law reserves for potentially permanent emplacement (Environment Code, Article L.
542-1-1).
High-level waste and long-lived intermediate-level waste, which together hold 99.9% of the declared radioactivity (end-2021), are stored on the sites that produce or hold them: their deep geological disposal, Cigéo, is planned at Bure; after the 2026 public inquiry and the inquiry commission’s favourable opinion, with two reservations, no creation licence decree had been published as of 12 November 2026.
Low- and intermediate-level short-lived waste and very-low-level waste, over nine tenths of the volume, have disposal facilities in operation.
Where do these figures come from?
From the open files of Andra’s National Inventory (waste declared at end-2024, and at end-2021 for activity), read by a script that stops if a figure no longer meets the conditions on which the page’s main sentences depend.
The status of disposal routes and the Malvési volumes are transcribed from Andra’s publications (Les Essentiels 2026, read on 26 October 2026).
The data can be downloaded as CSV and JSON, under a CC BY 4.0 licence.
