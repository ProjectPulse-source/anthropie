Does a French law apply as soon as it is passed?
Not by the vote alone.
Once the law has been promulgated and published, those of its provisions whose implementation requires decrees or ministerial orders wait for these measures.
On 5 October 2026, according to the barometer of the application of laws, 130 of the 399 laws promulgated from October 2017 to September 2025, excluding treaties (laws authorising the ratification or approval of an international agreement, or accession to one), were still waiting for at least one measure; 124 had received all of them and 144 were classified as having no measure to take.
A pending measure is not necessarily late, but the Government is not free to wait indefinitely: the Conseil d'État requires it to act within a reasonable time, and a refusal to adopt a measure that is due can be brought before the administrative courts, which can annul it and order the measure to be adopted.

A promise that goes through the law does not stop at the vote.
Between adoption and application, a French law goes through several steps, and some of its provisions then wait for decrees or ministerial orders (arrêtés).
This page follows these steps, counts the laws and the measures still pending for the laws promulgated since 2017 (by session as the Senate counts it: laws published from 1 October to 30 September), sets out what the courts require of the Government, and explains why the published implementation rates do not all say the same thing.
Counts of laws and measures are recalculated law by law from public sources; the rates published by the Senate and by the Government, transcribed from their reports, are reread by the script in the archived documents.
The Constitution is quoted in the English translation published by the Conseil constitutionnel; other French texts in French, followed by our translation.
From the vote to application: what steps does a law go through?
Five steps separate a law that has been passed from its application, and each has its own actor.
The vote.
Parliament gives the text its final adoption.
Promulgation.
The President of the Republic promulgates the law within fifteen days of its final passage and transmission to the Government, and may, within that time, ask Parliament to reopen debate on it (Constitution, art.
10).
The Constitutional Council steps in here: it must review Institutional Acts before their promulgation, other laws may be referred to it, and a referral suspends the time allotted for promulgation (art.
61).
Publication and entry into force.
Once published in the Journal officiel, the law enters into force on the date it sets or, failing that, the day after its publication (Civil Code, art.
1, para.
1).
The next paragraph adds: « Toutefois, l'entrée en vigueur de celles de leurs dispositions dont l'exécution nécessite des mesures d'application est reportée à la date d'entrée en vigueur de ces mesures. » [our translation: “However, provisions whose implementation requires implementing measures do not enter into force until those measures themselves enter into force.”] A third opens a faster route: « En cas d'urgence, entrent en vigueur dès leur publication les lois dont le décret de promulgation le prescrit » [our translation: “In case of urgency, laws whose promulgation decree so prescribes enter into force on their publication”].
Implementing measures.
The Prime Minister ensures the implementation of legislation and, subject to article 13, has power to make regulations (art.
21).
The President of the Republic signs the decrees deliberated upon in the Council of Ministers (art.
13), and the instruments of the Prime Minister are countersigned, where required, by the ministers responsible for their implementation (art.
22).
The law may also provide for ministerial orders, and a decree may itself refer matters to an order of a minister.
Implementation on the ground.
Then come the departments, budgets and local authorities that apply the rule.
This step lies beyond the data on this page: a published decree is not a result achieved.
Nor does the page say whether the necessary funds were made available, or what the local authorities responsible for applying certain laws do: no source was read on these two points.
The articles of the Constitution, word for word
Article 10.
“The President of the Republic shall promulgate Acts of Parliament within fifteen days following the final passage of an Act and its transmission to the Government.” “He may, before the expiry of this time limit, ask Parliament to reopen debate on the Act or any sections thereof.
Such reopening of debate shall not be refused.”
Article 61.
Institutional Acts are referred to the Constitutional Council before their promulgation (para.
1).
For the others, paragraph 2 provides: “Acts of Parliament may be referred to the Constitutional Council, before their promulgation, by the President of the Republic, the Prime Minister, the President of the National Assembly, the President of the Senate, sixty Members of the National Assembly or sixty Senators.” In these cases, “referral to the Constitutional Council shall suspend the time allotted for promulgation.”
Article 21, on the Prime Minister: “He shall ensure the implementation of legislation.” Subject to article 13, “he shall have power to make regulations”.
Article 13.
“The President of the Republic shall sign the Ordinances and Decrees deliberated upon in the Council of Ministers.”
Article 22.
“Instruments of the Prime Minister shall be countersigned, where required, by the ministers responsible for their implementation.”
The Civil Code does not cover every provision that announces a decree: it covers those whose implementation “requires” measures (« dont l’exécution nécessite »).
A law may refer to a decree for a provision that already applies without it.
In case of dispute, the court decides, provision by provision.
In 2021, on buildings for caged laying hens, the Conseil d’État thus held that « ces dispositions ne sont pas suffisamment précises pour permettre leur entrée en vigueur en l'absence du décret d'application dont elles prévoient d'ailleurs l'intervention » [our translation: “these provisions are not precise enough to allow their entry into force in the absence of the implementing decree, whose adoption they moreover provide for”] (CE, 27 May 2021, no.
441660).

The finding in one sentence.
On 5 October 2026, according to the barometer, 130 of the 399 laws promulgated in France from October 2017 to September 2025, excluding treaties, still had at least one measure classified as pending; 124 had received all their measures, 144 were classified as having no measure to take, and one had no schedule.
Measured against the laws classified with measures to take.
At the reading date, the barometer classifies 144 of the 399 laws promulgated as having no measure to take.
Of the others, 254 have a schedule that contains at least one measure to take, still pending or already taken, and one has no schedule.
Among these 254 laws, the 130 pending laws make up 51%, about one in two.
The law with no schedule is counted on neither side.
Both shares are accurate: one sets the pending laws against all the laws promulgated, the other against only the laws that the barometer classifies with at least one measure to take.
A category of the barometer is not an initial need.
The barometer classifies a law as having “no measure to take” when all its measures are classified as “not applicable” (sans objet) at the reading date: it is a current status, not proof that the law provided for no measure.
Law no.
2025-138 on amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is the counter-example: classified as having no measure to take, it provided for a ministerial order, since adopted, whose reference is given in the barometer’s observation (« Arrêté du 5 février 2026 »; see the next section).
The ratio of 130 to 254 therefore compares two categories of the barometer; it does not say what share of the laws that provided for a measure are still waiting for one.
What these two shares do not say.
They say neither that these 130 laws were wholly inapplicable, nor that each of their pending measures was late.
A law may apply for the most part while waiting for a decree on one point, and the law itself may defer a measure.
The planned date is missing for 525 of the 567 pending measures: without a date, the page cannot say whether a measure is late.
Age does not settle everything: for the 2017-2018 session, the oldest, 8 laws out of 41 are still waiting for at least one measure.
The Senate as a check.
The Senate, for its part, counts “fully applicable” laws: those directly applicable and those that have received all the planned measures.
For the 2024-2025 session, it writes: « Au 31 mars 2026, 36 lois (soit 64 %) adoptées au cours de la session 2024-25 sur 56 étaient entièrement applicables. » [our translation: “As at 31 March 2026, 36 laws (64%) adopted during the 2024-25 session out of 56 were fully applicable.”] It adds: « Plus précisément, 22 d'entre elles (soit 39 %) étaient d'application directe et 14 (soit 25 %) avaient fait l'objet de l'intégralité des mesures d'application prévues. » [our translation: “More precisely, 22 of them (39%) were directly applicable and 14 (25%) had received all the planned implementing measures.”] The barometer also counts 56 laws for this session.
On 5 October 2026, these same two categories cover 34 of them: 20 with no measure to take and 14 whose measures have all been taken.
These are two findings, on two dates, by two producers that do not classify laws in the same way: they neither add up nor confirm each other, and nothing establishes that the laws counted on each side are the same.

On 5 October 2026, of the 5,398 measures listed for these laws, 4,378 have an identified published instrument, 567 are pending, 448 are classified as “not applicable” (sans objet) and 5 have an incomplete status (“applied” with no identified instrument).
For the laws of 2017-2018, 18 measures out of 556 remain pending; for those of 2024-2025, 119 out of 521.
“Pending” does not mean “late”.
116 of the 567 pending measures carry, in their subject or their observation, the word « différé » or « différée » (deferred), 6 of them for the laws of 2018-2019 and 14 for those of 2019-2020; the word does not say whether the deferred date has passed.
The planned date is missing for 525 of the 567 pending measures: how long a measure has been waiting therefore cannot be calculated, and the page publishes no time frame.
“Not applicable” is a label, not a proof.
The producer files 448 measures under this label and gives the reason in an observation.
Sorted by written rules, these observations most often say that the measure is already implemented by an existing text (153), that its legal basis was repealed, rewritten or stripped of its reference to a decree (96), or that no measure is needed (41); 16 concern optional measures that were not used, four say that a decree was not adopted, and 43 remain undetermined, since they do not fall under a single rule.
This sorting repeats what the producer writes; it reclassifies no measure.
Of the 448 “not applicable” observations, there remain the 95 that begin with the reference of an order or a decree.
For the laws of 2024-2025, they number 91.
The three observations that begin with « Décret non pris » (decree not adopted), with no reference to an instrument, are not counted among them.
For law no.
2025-138, the observation reads « Arrêté du 5 février 2026 » (an arrêté is a ministerial order), and the Government’s report at 31 December 2025 counts its measure as pending: the two sources describe two states, on two dates, and do not contradict each other.
What was checked, and how
The measures are those of the barometer of the application of laws, published by the National Assembly and LexImpact from DILA’s implementation schedules, in its version of 5 October 2026.
A measure counts as published if its status is “applied” and an instrument is identified; “applied” without an identified instrument gives an incomplete status.
The script checks that each measure falls into one and only one category, session by session, and that the same producer’s file of laws gives, law by law, the same number of measures applied and to be applied; it also checks that each law falls under one and only one of the four statuses.
A second program, written separately, recounted the categories of measures from the raw file: the numbers match.
The rules used to sort the “not applicable” observations are published in the dataset; an observation that falls under none or several of the rules is undetermined.
An observation counts as carrying the reference of an instrument if it begins with « Arrêté du », « Décret du » or « Décret n° »; those that begin with the word but no reference are listed in the dataset, with their reason.
The barometer only publishes the current status.
DILA’s open data on implementation schedules only go back to July 2025.
No “six months after the law” rate can therefore be recalculated for earlier sessions, and the page publishes none.
Can a law apply without any decree?
Yes.
The barometer and the Senate, in its annual reports, each classify laws as directly applicable, but not always the same ones.
The barometer classifies a law this way when its schedule contains no measure other than “not applicable”; the Senate applies its own rules.
Matched law by law over the 399 laws promulgated from 2017-2018 to 2024-2025, excluding 154 international treaties, the two sources keep exactly the same scope, but classify 24 laws differently.

In seven sessions out of eight, directly applicable laws stay below half, whatever the source.
In 2018-2019, the barometer counts 25 out of 49, more than half, while the Senate counts 22.
The 2024-2025 gap can be read within a single Senate report.
Its summary says: « Plus précisément, 22 d'entre elles (soit 39 %) étaient d'application directe » [our translation: “More precisely, 22 of them (39%) were directly applicable”]; another page states that « le nombre de lois promulguées lors de la session 2024-2025 s'établit à 56, dont 24 d'application directe » [our translation: “the number of laws promulgated during the 2024-2025 session stands at 56, of which 24 directly applicable”].
The barometer counts 20.
The gap between 20 and 22 is explained law by law by different classification rules; the one between 22 and 24 is not, for lack of a list of names.
Why the two sources diverge on 24 laws
The Senate classifies laws in the annexes to its reports.
Several rules explain part of the gaps with the barometer: in some years, the Senate leaves out the measures that the law makes optional; the barometer classifies as “directly applicable” a law all of whose measures are now classified as “not applicable”; it classifies as “not directly applicable” a law with no schedule.
Applied together, these rules remove some gaps and create others: they are not applied uniformly from one year to the next.
All the laws in a gap are counted as uncertain, even when a rule explains them; hence the bounds in the table.
Defects in the Senate’s annexes, such as a wrong law number or an announced count that does not match the list that follows, were corrected using the title and date of the law; the law-by-law register flags them.
Does the Government have a deadline to adopt the decrees?
Articles 13 and 21 of the Constitution set no deadline.
The Government sets itself a six-month deadline, according to the Senate (see the series below).
The courts, for their part, impose an obligation: « L'exercice du pouvoir réglementaire comporte non seulement le droit mais aussi l'obligation de prendre dans un délai raisonnable les mesures qu'implique nécessairement l'application de la loi, hors le cas où le respect d'engagements internationaux de la France y ferait obstacle. » [our translation: “The exercise of regulatory power entails not only the right but also the obligation to take, within a reasonable time, the measures that the application of the law necessarily implies, except where compliance with France's international commitments would prevent it.”] (Conseil d’État, 27 May 2021, no.
441660; the same wording appears in its decisions of 29 June 2011 and 21 January 2021).
It also applies to the ministerial order to which an implementing decree refers certain measures (CE, 21 January 2021, no.
429956).
The reasonable time is not set once and for all: it is assessed « eu égard à la nature des mesures à prendre » [our translation: “having regard to the nature of the measures to be taken”] (CE, 27 July 2005, no.
261694).
Periods held excessive in one case do not therefore make a rule.
A refusal to adopt a measure that is due can be brought before the administrative courts, which can annul it and order the measure to be adopted: in May 2021, the Conseil d’État ordered the Prime Minister to adopt a decree, and in January 2021 it ordered the Minister of Justice to adopt an order, each time within a period it set.
The delay may also make the State liable and give rise to compensation (CE, 27 July 2005).
A planned measure is not always due.
In 2011, about an order provided for by a decree and never adopted, the Conseil d’État held that the minister was not bound to adopt it: the article that provided for this order already set out exhaustively the content of the file in question, and referred to the order only the model of that file (CE, 29 June 2011, no.
343188).
The Conseil d’État’s decisions, word for word
Orders adopted on referral from a decree (CE, 21 January 2021, no.
429956): « Lorsqu'un décret pris pour l'application d'une loi renvoie lui-même à un arrêté la détermination de certaines mesures nécessaires à cette application, cet arrêté doit également intervenir dans un délai raisonnable. » [our translation: “Where a decree adopted for the application of a law itself refers to a ministerial order the determination of certain measures necessary for that application, that order must also be issued within a reasonable time.”]
Injunctions.
In May 2021, the Conseil d’État decided « d'enjoindre au Premier ministre de prendre ce décret dans un délai de six mois à compter de la notification de la présente décision » [our translation: “to order the Prime Minister to adopt this decree within six months of notification of this decision”] (no.
441660); in January 2021, « d'ordonner au garde des sceaux, ministre de la justice, de prendre l'arrêté prévu par l'article 9 du décret du 29 juin 2020 dans un délai de trois mois » [our translation: “to order the Keeper of the Seals, Minister of Justice, to adopt the order provided for by article 9 of the decree of 29 June 2020 within three months”] (no.
429956).
Compensation (CE, 27 July 2005, no.
261694): « les préjudices qui résultent du retard mis à prendre, au-delà d'un délai raisonnable, un décret nécessaire à l'application d'une loi ouvrent droit à réparation » [our translation: “the losses resulting from the delay in adopting, beyond a reasonable time, a decree necessary for the application of a law give rise to compensation”].
A planned measure that was not due (CE, 29 June 2011, no.
343188).
The Conseil d’État notes that « l'article R.
1243-4 fixe de façon exhaustive et précise le contenu du dossier de demande d'autorisation » [our translation: “article R.
1243-4 sets out exhaustively and precisely the content of the authorisation application file”], and concludes that « le ministre chargé de la santé n'était pas tenu de prendre l'arrêté prévu par ce dernier article » [our translation: “the minister responsible for health was not bound to adopt the order provided for by that article”].
What these decisions do not say.
They lay down a rule and settle cases; they say neither how many refusals are brought before the courts nor what becomes of the orders issued, and no data read here measures it.
Nor does the barometer’s “pending” status say whether a measure is due, or whether the reasonable time has been exceeded.
Why do the French Senate and Government publish different rates?
Because their counting conventions have differed and still differ in part.
The gap has a history.
In 2012-2013, rates that matched.
The Senate writes that, « pour les lois promulguées durant l'année parlementaire 2012-2013, le taux d'application des lois s'élève à 90 % (si on compte en nombre de lois) ou à environ 64 % (si on compte en nombre de mesures), pourcentages calculés à partir de la base statistique du Sénat et qui recoupent parfaitement ceux calculés par les services du Gouvernement » [our translation: “for the laws promulgated during the 2012-2013 parliamentary year, the implementation rate of laws stands at 90% (counting by number of laws) or about 64% (counting by number of measures), percentages calculated from the Senate's statistical database and which match exactly those calculated by the Government's departments”].
That year, the rate changed depending on whether laws or measures were counted, not on who produced it.
In 2017-2019, a gap and its reasons.
For 2017-2018, once deferred measures are set aside, the Senate’s and the Government’s rates come closer, 86% for the Senate and 85% for the Government; ministerial orders, which only the Senate counted, remain a difference of scope.
For 2018-2019, the Senate publishes 72% and reports that the Government reaches 82.4%.
In 2019, the Senate itself gave two technical reasons for the gap, ministerial orders and deferred measures, and a third ground, which it called political reasons, in particular when it considers that a decree adopted does not respect the legislature’s intent: the expected measure then counts for it as not taken.
A rate can thus include a judgement, and not only a count.
What the Senate and the Government wrote in 2018 and 2019, word for word
For 2017-2018, the Senate writes: « Il atteint désormais 78 % - 86 % si on exclut les mesures dont l'entrée en vigueur est différée » [our translation: “It now stands at 78% - 86% if measures whose entry into force is deferred are excluded”].
The General Secretariat of the Government, heard by the Senate, states: « Au 31 mars 2019, ces 28 lois appelaient 461 mesures réglementaires d'application et nous en avons pris 393, soit un taux d'application de 85 % » [our translation: “On 31 March 2019, these 28 laws called for 461 implementing regulations and we adopted 393 of them, an implementation rate of 85%”].
For 2018-2019, the Senate writes: « Le taux global d'application des lois calculé par le Sénat est de 72 % » [our translation: “The overall implementation rate of laws calculated by the Senate is 72%”] and adds that « les services du Secrétariat général du Gouvernement parviennent pour leur part à un taux global de 82,4 % » [our translation: “the departments of the General Secretariat of the Government arrive for their part at an overall rate of 82.4%”].
In 2019, it gave the reasons: « Le taux calculé par le Sénat peut diverger de celui calculé par le Gouvernement pour des raisons techniques, qui sont de deux ordres. » [our translation: “The rate calculated by the Senate may differ from the one calculated by the Government for technical reasons, of two kinds.”] First, « le Sénat effectue un contrôle global des mesures d'application des lois, en incluant les arrêtés dont la publication est prévue par la loi.
Ce n'est pas le cas du secrétariat général du Gouvernement. » [our translation: “the Senate carries out a comprehensive check of the implementing measures of laws, including the ministerial orders (arrêtés) whose publication is provided for by the law.
This is not the case of the General Secretariat of the Government.”] Second, « le Sénat intègre dans son taux les mesures attendues pour des articles dont l'entrée en vigueur est différée, à la différence du Gouvernement. » [our translation: “the Senate includes in its rate the measures expected for articles whose entry into force is deferred, unlike the Government.”] It also mentions a third ground: « Il peut également diverger pour des raisons politiques, notamment lorsque le Sénat considère qu'un décret pris ne respecte pas la volonté du législateur, et donc que la mesure attendue n'est pas prise. » [our translation: “It may also differ for political reasons, in particular when the Senate considers that a decree adopted does not reflect the legislature's intent, and therefore that the expected measure has not been taken.”]
Since 2024, methods brought closer.
The Senate’s 2026 report says so: « Depuis le 1er janvier 2024, le SGG contrôle la parution des arrêtés sous réserve qu'ils aient été expressément prévus par une disposition législative. » [our translation: “Since 1 January 2024, the SGG has checked the publication of ministerial orders provided that they were expressly provided for by a legislative provision.”] For the Senate itself, « il a été décidé, pour la session 2024-2025, de ne plus prendre en compte les mesures différées au-delà du 31 mars 2026 » [our translation: “it was decided, for the 2024-2025 session, no longer to take into account measures deferred beyond 31 March 2026”].
It adds: « En dépit de la convergence des méthodes, certaines divergences demeurent quant à l'appréciation de l'effectivité de l'application de certaines lois entre le Sénat et le SGG. » [our translation: “Despite the convergence of methods, some divergences remain between the Senate and the SGG as to the assessment of whether certain laws are effectively applied.”]
The rates published today.
At 31 December 2025, the Government’s half-yearly report on the 16th legislature « fait apparaître le taux d'application au 31 décembre 2025 des lois qui, parmi celles votées entre le 1er juillet 2022 et le 9 juin 2024, appellent des décrets d'application. » [our translation: “shows the implementation rate at 31 December 2025 of the laws which, among those passed between 1 July 2022 and 9 June 2024, call for implementing decrees.”] It gives a rate of 87%, over 1,088 measures.
The report on the 17th legislature « fait apparaître le taux d'application au 31 décembre 2025 des lois qui, parmi celles publiées entre le 18 juillet 2024 et le 30 juin 2025, appellent des décrets ou des arrêtés d'application. » [our translation: “shows the implementation rate at 31 December 2025 of the laws which, among those published between 18 July 2024 and 30 June 2025, call for implementing decrees or orders.”] It gives 55%, over 320 measures.
The Senate publishes 66% for the 2024-2025 session.
These three rates cover neither the same laws, nor the same measures, nor the same date: the table below sets them out by definition, it does not rank them.

Has the implementation rate of laws risen since 2002?
The Senate’s series does not allow us to say so from end to end.
The corpus shows six successive definitions since 2002-2003, which differ in cut-off date, scope of laws and treatment of deferred and optional measures: the segments do not read as a single series.
The Senate nevertheless reads a trend across its own breaks: « le Sénat assiste depuis 2018 à une réduction globale et constante du taux d'application moyen des lois qu'il adopte » [our translation: “since 2018 the Senate has witnessed an overall and steady decline in the average implementation rate of the laws it adopts”].
Its series only allows this reading to be tested segment by segment: 2024-2025 falls under a different definition from the previous sessions.

The last point of the first segment and the first point kept from the next are separated by a change of definition: in order to count the measures taken only once the time limit the Government sets itself has expired, the Senate then shifts the period it uses.
In 2024-2025, it publishes a single rate (« Taux d'application global des lois 66 % (+ 4 points) » [our translation: “Overall implementation rate of laws 66% (+ 4 points)”]), and specifies in a note: « Les données prises en compte sont celles des mesures prévues, non éventuelles et non différées au-delà du 31 mars 2026, par les dispositions législatives. » [our translation: “The data taken into account are those of the measures provided for by the legislative provisions, excluding optional measures and measures deferred beyond 31 March 2026.”] Optional measures (mesures éventuelles) are those the law allows without requiring them.
The “+ 4 points” cannot be read in the table, which gives 59% for 2023-2024: the Senate compares with its 2023-2024 rate excluding deferred measures.
The previous report said so: « Si l'on exclut les mesures dont le législateur a autorisé une entrée en vigueur différée, ce taux d'application s'élève à 62 % » [our translation: “If measures whose deferred entry into force the legislature has authorised are excluded, this implementation rate stands at 62%”].
For 2019-2020, the reports give two values, that of the 2020 report and that of the following report, marked with an open circle in the figure.
A revised figure or another variant of the rate: the documents do not settle it, and the series keeps both values.
The 2009-2010 session is not shown in the figure, for lack of a cut-off date established in the sentence that gives its rate.
Two values for the same session and a change of definition are two distinct facts: the series keeps both.
The change of definition and the two 2019-2020 values, word for word
The break between the first two segments, according to the Senate: « cette année, pour la première fois, afin de ne mesurer le nombre de mesures réglementaires prises qu'à l'expiration du délai de six mois que s'impose à lui-même le Gouvernement, la période retenue comprend trois mois de moins pour les lois promulguées retenues et trois mois de plus pour les mesures réglementaires prises. » [our translation: “this year, for the first time, in order to measure the number of regulations adopted only at the end of the six-month period the Government sets itself, the period used includes three months fewer for the laws promulgated and three months more for the regulations adopted.”]
For 2019-2020, the 2020 report says: « Le taux global d'application des lois mesuré par le Sénat cette année est de 62 % » [our translation: “The overall implementation rate of laws measured by the Senate this year is 62%”].
The next one, on 2020-2021, writes « soit un taux inférieur à celui de l'année précédente (60 %) » [our translation: “a lower rate than that of the previous year (60%)”], then: « En incluant les mesures d'application prises mais non prévues, ce taux est de 63 % (62 % pour l'année précédente) » [our translation: “Including implementing measures adopted but not planned, this rate is 63% (62% for the previous year)”].
What this page does not say
It does not say whether a law produces the effect the promise aimed at.
It does not measure the quality of decrees, nor whether they reflect the legislature’s intent, which the Senate assesses itself.
It gives no publication time, for lack of a dated history of the schedules.
It sets the Senate’s and the Government’s rates side by side with their definitions, without ranking them, and does not join two segments of the Senate’s series.

Frequently asked questions
Does a French law apply as soon as it is published in the Journal officiel?
In principle, it enters into force on the date it sets or, failing that, the day after its publication; in case of urgency, on its publication if the promulgation decree so prescribes (Civil Code, art.
1).
But provisions whose implementation requires implementing measures do not enter into force until those measures do (our translation).
A law can therefore be in force in part and wait for its decrees or orders in another part.
How many French laws are still waiting for an implementing measure?
On 5 October 2026, according to the barometer of the application of laws, 130 of the 399 laws promulgated from October 2017 to September 2025, excluding treaties, still had at least one measure classified as pending; 124 had received all their measures, 144 were classified as having no measure to take and one had no schedule.
Of the 5,398 measures listed for these laws, 567 were pending.
A pending measure is not necessarily late.
What is the implementation rate of laws in France?
It depends on counting conventions.
At 31 December 2025, the General Secretariat of the Government publishes 87% for the laws of the 16th legislature, decrees only, and 55% for those of the 17th, decrees or orders; the Senate publishes 66% for the 2024-2025 session, at 31 March 2026.
These rates cover neither the same laws, nor the same measures, nor the same date: they cannot be ranked.
Is one law in two left unapplied?
On 5 October 2026, according to the barometer, 130 of the 399 laws promulgated from October 2017 to September 2025, excluding treaties, still had at least one measure classified as pending.
Measured against the 254 laws whose schedule contained at least one measure to take, pending or taken, they make up 51%, about one in two: this is a ratio between two categories of the barometer at the reading date, not the share of pending laws among those that provided for a measure.
This means neither that these laws were wholly inapplicable, nor that each of these measures was late.
A phrase like "one law in two" may come from this second count, from a rate per measure, from a single session or from another date: before repeating it, one must know what it counts, on what date and for which laws.

The figures, quotations, checks and charts are produced by a single script, which stops if a piece of data no longer meets the conditions on which the main sentences rest.
Each quotation is checked word for word in the archived document it comes from: pages of the Senate’s reports, articles of the Constitution and of the Civil Code, decisions of the Conseil d’État; for the report of the General Secretariat of the Government, which Légifrance refuses to scripts, in an extract of the page read through a browser, whose fingerprint is the one the browser computed.
Counts of laws and measures are recalculated from the barometer’s files; the Senate’s rates, transcribed in the script, are reread in the archived pages of its reports; the sorting of the “not applicable” observations follows rules written in the script.
