Why does French public debt rise?
Between 1995 and 2025, French public debt went from 57.8% to 115.7% of GDP: it doubled as a share of the wealth produced.
Of these 57.9 points of GDP, public deficits excluding interest, known as primary deficits, contributed 50.5.
This balance shows which terms drove the debt up, not why the deficits existed.

France is a telling case.
Its debt ratio doubled in thirty years, and Eurostat’s harmonised series allow that rise to be split, year by year, into the terms of a standard accounting identity.
The accounting identity is general; the estimates below are for France.
How does the rise in the debt-to-GDP ratio break down?
Cumulative decomposition of the change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, France, 1995-2025, in points of GDP (Eurostat).
Blue: the stock; orange: what pushes it up; grey: what pulls it down.
The result in one sentence.
Interest (73.9 points) and nominal GDP growth (−72.6) almost offset each other: their net effect is only +1.3 points over thirty years, but it is the sum of two opposite periods, +16.9 points before 2017 and −15.6 since; stock-flow adjustments, which do not go through the deficit, add 6.1 points.
How can interest that is paid “cancel out”?
It does not: it is a real charge; other things being equal, it raises the borrowing requirement and adds to the debt.
But the page measures a ratio, debt relative to the wealth produced in the year, and that wealth grows too.
Take a debt equal to GDP and isolate interest, with no other spending, revenue or financial transaction, financing it with new borrowing: if the year’s interest raises the debt by one fiftieth and GDP also rises by one fiftieth, the debt has risen in euros, but the ratio has not moved.
The interest has not disappeared: it is the growth of the denominator that offsets its effect on the ratio.
The 73.9 points mostly “erased” by growth are therefore contributions, cumulated over thirty years, to the change in a ratio, not an amount paid equal to 73.9% of GDP.
Who receives this interest and who bears what the debt costs is the subject of Who really pays for public debt?

1996-2007: the interest-growth effect.
The ratio gains 7.7 points.
The implicit interest rate on the debt, that is, the average rate on the debt as a whole, then exceeds nominal growth on average: the interest-growth effect adds 7.5 points, while the primary balance, close to equilibrium and sometimes in surplus, contributes −2.1.
2008-2019: primary deficits.
The ratio gains 32.7 points, of which +26.9 come from primary deficits and +6.1 from the interest-growth effect.
The year 2009 alone accounts for 14.2 points: the primary deficit widens, GDP contracts and stock-flow adjustments weigh in too, three movements that coincide with the financial crisis.
2020-2025: nominal growth holds the ratio back, deficits push it up.
The ratio gains 17.5 points.
In 2020 it jumps by 16.7 points, with the pandemic.
From 2021 to 2023, strong nominal GDP growth, price rises included, brings the ratio down: the interest-growth effect takes 16.8 points off the ratio, more than primary deficits add (11.5).

Since 2024, the ratio has been rising again.
The implicit interest rate has been rising since its low of 2020, mostly through a jump in 2022.
After 1.25% in 2020, it edges up in 2021 (1.31%), jumps in 2022 (1.80%), then rises more slowly.
That jump does not come only from bonds being renewed at the new rates: in 2022, inflation also sharply raised the recorded cost of the State’s inflation-linked bonds, according to the French Senate’s report on that year’s accounts.
The accounts presented here do not make it possible to separate the share of each mechanism in the implicit rate of general government as a whole.
The 2025 accounts are still subject to revision.
Those on this page are the Eurostat series released on 21 October 2026, for the debt (with the October notification) as for the accounts.
INSEE publishes the same debt for the end of 2025 as that notification, 115.7% of GDP; the page keeps the Eurostat series throughout, so that the decomposition stays consistent.
That year, the implicit rate (1.99%) and nominal growth (1.90%) are almost equal, and the interest-growth effect is close to zero: so narrow a gap does not say which way the effect will go next.
Over the 30 years of the series, the primary balance was in surplus only 4 times.
This count describes a recurrence; it does not say whether each of these deficits was desirable, avoidable or cyclical.
Too much spending or too little revenue?
The primary deficit is the gap between spending excluding interest and public revenue.
It does not say whether that gap comes from rising spending, falling revenue or both at once.
To describe it, one has to pick two years and compare spending and revenue, as a share of GDP, between them.
Eurostat’s series then give different answers depending on the years chosen.
From 2017 to 2024, the period chosen by the OFCE: public revenue changes by −3.1 points of GDP, spending excluding interest by −0.9.
The fall in revenue dominates.
From 2019 to 2025, the period chosen by the French Treasury: spending excluding interest changes by +1.3 points, revenue by −0.8.
The rise in spending dominates.
From 2019 to 2024, the window common to both: spending excluding interest changes by +1.2 points, revenue by −1.8.
The fall in revenue dominates again.
The diagnosis depends on both ends.
It changes with the start year, but also with the end year: ending in 2024 rather than 2025, the same start year gives the opposite conclusion.
Two accurate comparisons over different periods can therefore lead to opposite descriptive findings — neither, on its own, establishes the causes of the deficits or identifies the policy that should have been pursued.
The page measures one Eurostat indicator over these windows; it does not treat the OFCE’s and the Treasury’s work as equivalent, since they also differ in their definitions and questions.
Faced with either, the first question to ask is “from which year to which year?”.
Yet two equally accurate windows are not equally relevant: a period is justified by the question asked (the effect of decisions taken since a given date, the situation before a crisis), not by the result it gives.
What reverses the conclusion between the two windows starting in 2019 is revenue.
Spending excluding interest changes by the same amount in both, to within rounding; revenue, by contrast, rises by 1.0 point of GDP in 2025.
Over 2019-2025, the rise in spending outweighs the fall in revenue by only 0.5 points: a revision of the accounts of that size would be enough to reverse the finding.
The finding over 2017-2024, which ends before 2025, does not depend on that revision.
It is the easing of the fall in revenue, not a movement in spending, that tips their relative weight.
These movements are measured as shares of GDP: they can come from the numerator, the denominator, or both.
And a shrinking deficit has not disappeared: in 2025, the primary balance is still in deficit and the debt keeps rising.
These comparisons, on accounts still subject to revision, do not say which decisions produced these movements, or what should be corrected.
The conclusions the OFCE and the French Treasury draw from them, and the objects they examine, are compared below.
Business cycle or structure?
A deficit can stem from the business cycle: when activity stays below its potential level (the level it could sustain without pressure on prices), revenue weakens and some spending rises, then the deficit shrinks with the recovery.
It can also be structural, and remain once the economy is back at its potential.
Three institutions estimate this split, each with its own method: the European Commission, the IMF and the OECD.
Cyclically adjusted primary balance according to three estimates (IMF from 1996, Commission and OECD from 2010), and observed primary balance (Eurostat).
The grey bands mark the two periods compared.
Over 2022-2025, the deficit would not disappear with the business cycle alone.
Cyclically adjusted, the primary balance is lower over 2022-2025 than over 2015-2019 in all three estimates: by 1.9 points according to the Commission (−1.1% then −3.0% of potential GDP), by 2.6 points according to the IMF (−0.5% then −3.1%) and by 1.6 points according to the OECD (−1.8% then −3.4%).
Over 2022-2025, the gap, calculated here, between the observed and the cyclically adjusted balances that the IMF publishes in the same edition is 0.23 points, one as a share of GDP, the other of potential GDP.
These are three estimates, and they do not coincide.
The Commission and the OECD also remove one-off measures (exceptional revenue or spending that does not recur), the IMF does not; and each relies on an estimate of potential output, which is not a measurement and gets revised.
The page gives the three values, without averaging them.
On average over 1996-2007, the cyclically adjusted balance was close to zero.
Of the three balances in the chart, only the IMF’s is plotted before 2010; the OECD publishes its underlying balance from 1995, and the Commission its cyclically adjusted balance from 1978.
Its cyclically adjusted primary balance averages −0.2% of potential GDP over 1996-2007, −2.1% over 2010-2014, −0.5% over 2015-2019, then −3.1% over 2022-2025.
That average combines surpluses in the late 1990s and deficits from 2002: the adjusted deficit predates the 2008 crisis; deepened after it, it was partly absorbed, and the one after 2020 has not been so far.
This sequence dates the deteriorations; it is not enough to establish that the crises produced them.
Where: revenue over the recent period, spending over the long run.
The cyclically adjusted revenue and non-interest spending published by the Commission add up exactly to its adjusted balance.
From 2015-2019 to 2022-2025, that balance falls by 1.70 points: adjusted revenue falls by 1.62 points, adjusted spending changes by +0.09 points.
The structural balance cited above falls by 1.9 points, a gap that may come from its also removing one-off measures, present above all in 2019.
Since 1996-2007, by contrast, adjusted spending has risen by 3.9 points of potential GDP and revenue by 0.9: over the long run, the rise in spending dominates.
As with the OFCE and Treasury windows above, the answer depends on the years compared.
What this split does not say.
Cyclically adjusted revenue that falls mixes decided tax cuts and the spontaneous yield of taxes, which can grow more slowly than the economy.
These series do not separate the two: they say where the deficit deepened, and that the business cycle alone does not explain it, not which decisions deepened it.
The Court of Audit’s accounts separate them; the next section sets them against the Eurostat and INSEE series, and shows that part of this fall in revenue has a counterpart in spending.

Why Eurostat’s observed balance differs from the IMF’s.
This page’s primary balance excludes interest paid; the IMF’s excludes net interest, paid minus received.
The gap between the two is therefore the interest received by general government: 0.37 points of GDP on average over 1996-2007 and 0.13 points over 2022-2025, to within a few hundredths.
It is essentially a difference of definition.

Tax cuts, or revenue that slows down?
Revenue can fall as a share of GDP because the law cut it, or because, with the law unchanged, it grows more slowly than the economy.
Each year, the French Court of Audit (Cour des comptes) separates these two movements for compulsory levies (taxes and social contributions): their spontaneous growth, at constant legislation, and new measures, the increases or cuts that were decided, as costed by the administration.
Accumulated since 2015 and set against the two periods of the previous section, its series make it possible to test one reading of the recent deficit: lasting tax cuts whose cost would have stayed hidden until 2023 behind buoyant revenue.
More than a third of the fall in observed revenue comes from how refundable tax credits are recorded, and it has a counterpart in spending.
These credits can be paid out beyond the tax due.
Under European rules, Eurostat, like the Commission, therefore records taxes before these credits and classifies the credits as spending; INSEE and the Court of Audit compute compulsory levies net of them.
The largest of them, the tax credit for competitiveness and employment (CICE), was replaced in 2019 by a permanent cut in employer social contributions: gross revenue fell, and so did tax-credit spending.
The tax credits that Eurostat adds to revenue go from 1.4% of GDP over 2015-2019 to 0.8% over 2022-2025.
Net of these credits, compulsory levies fall by 1.0 point of GDP, not by 1.6 like total revenue, cyclically adjusted or not; the remainder, a reconciliation gap between these series, changes by +0.02 points.
Removed from observed spending excluding interest as well, the same series brings its rise to about 0.9 points of GDP instead of 0.3.
That figure remains an estimate: in its current accounts, on the 2020 base, INSEE records tax-credit spending in the year the entitlement arises, and the revenue in the year the credit is set against tax.
The balance itself does not change: what changes is the split between revenue and spending.

The masking, then the normalisation.
From 2017 to 2022, elasticity almost exactly offsets the cuts that were decided, whose amounts the Court of Audit details; among the largest: abolition of the housing tax (EUR 15.7 billion), cuts in production taxes (EUR 11.3 billion) and in the corporate income tax rate (EUR 11.1 billion), reform of the income tax schedule (EUR 5.4 billion).
The 2022 levy rate, 45.0% of GDP, is thus close to that of 2017, 45.3%.
Then elasticity turns and takes away 2.1 points in 2023 and 2024: the decided cuts are then no longer offset in the levy rate.
The Court of Audit wrote as early as July 2024 that this spontaneous buoyancy “was still masking, at the end of 2022, the effect of the cuts in compulsory levies made since 2018” (our translation).
From 2024 to 2025, new measures change direction and bring back 0.9 points.
Those of 2024 include a EUR 3.9 billion cut in banks’ contributions to the European Single Resolution Fund, with no effect on the government balance; excluding these levies earmarked for Europe, they reach EUR 7.1 billion.
The total of measures since 2015 goes from −2.5 in 2023 to −1.6 in 2025.
Nearly EUR 8.5 billion of the 2025 increases, or 0.3 points of GDP, come from schemes announced as temporary, two of which were extended in 2026.
What this test establishes, and what it leaves open.
The mechanism is described by the Court of Audit itself: cuts in levies, mostly lasting, offset until 2022 by spontaneously buoyant revenue, whose effect appeared in 2023 and 2024.
The test measures its scope between the two periods.
Of the fall in observed revenue, 1.6 points, 0.6 comes from the fall in refundable tax credits, mostly the end of the CICE, which has its counterpart in spending.
Of the net fall of 1.0 point, measures, the CICE and elasticity leave a closing gap of −0.14.
This is an accounting breakdown, not a causal attribution: the administration costs the measures mostly with behaviour unchanged, leaving out their effect on activity and on tax bases, and these effects blend into so-called spontaneous growth.
Some measures are temporary, others have no effect on the government balance.
Finally, the test describes a change between two periods; it does not say why the deficit was already high before.
How the calculation works
Each year, the debt-to-GDP ratio moves under the effect of three terms.
The interest-growth effect: the year’s interest adds to the debt, while nominal GDP growth — real growth plus price rises — lowers the ratio even without any repayment; their balance is positive when the implicit interest rate exceeds nominal growth, negative in the opposite case.
The primary deficit: the gap between public spending excluding interest and public revenue, which measures its size, not its cause.
Stock-flow adjustments: debt that moves without passing through the deficit (cash set aside, loans and equity stakes, valuation effects), obtained here as a residual.
The waterfall shows the interest-growth effect as its two components, interest and growth: hence its four steps for three terms.

What this decomposition does not say
An accounting decomposition, not a causal explanation.
It identifies which accounting components moved the debt; it does not say why the deficits existed, whether they were avoidable, or who is responsible for them.
Contributions, not amounts paid.
The points of GDP in the waterfall measure the effect on a ratio, cumulated over thirty years; they do not say how much was paid, to whom, or when.
Terms that are not independent.
In a recession, revenue falls and some spending rises: the primary deficit itself depends on growth.
Inflation does not reduce the real burden of nominal debt without cost.
It reduces the real value of nominal claims, and that loss is borne by someone: this is the subject of Who really pays for public debt?
Nothing about the future.
What comes next depends on the gap between the implicit interest rate and nominal growth, and on the primary balance: what it took elsewhere for debt to come down is the subject of Can public debt come down?

Measured here.
The annual decomposition of the French ratio from 1995 to 2025, on Eurostat series, checked against the published ratios.
None of the texts read decomposes this window with this source: the measurement is validated by reproduction, not by citation.
Consistent with.
The 2014 citizens’ audit attributed “a good third” of the rise in the debt from 1980 to 2012 to the effect of interest rates, which it calls the “snowball” effect and this page calls the interest-growth effect, and “nearly two thirds” to accumulated primary deficits (our translation).
The two analyses share neither the same end points nor necessarily the same counterfactual (the reference situation used for comparison); over their common period, from end-1995 to end-2012, the interest-growth effect weighs 14.7 points out of 33.9 here, ahead of primary deficits (13.1) and stock-flow adjustments (6.2), which the audit does not show separately: taken together, these last two terms come first.
The comparison of their methods is set out in the working paper AWP-09.
Over 1970-2023, using INSEE accounts and a historical database, Auclert, Philippon and Ragot find that primary deficits and stock-flow adjustments account for 88 points of a rise of nearly 90 (from 21% to 110% of GDP), and a “snowball” effect close to zero on average: the same identity, another period, another source, and a primary deficit that includes the adjustments this page isolates.
Clavères, at the French Treasury, finds the same sequence of signs: the effective interest rate on the debt moves above nominal growth in the course of the 1980s, and growth moves back above it from 2016.
That rate has the same definition as this page’s implicit interest rate (interest relative to the debt at the end of the previous year), but it is computed on other series, historical and international databases including the IMF’s.
The OFCE (Heyer, Plane, Ragot, Sampognaro and Timbeau) links France’s 53-point rise in debt from 2000 to 2024 (against 44 points in Spain, 27 in Italy and 5 in Germany) to deficits, without decomposing these rises.
The French Treasury describes debt rising almost continuously since 2001, with deficit reductions in favourable periods only stabilising the ratio, and a deficit that stayed above the stabilising balance (the deficit that would keep the ratio constant) until 2019.
It describes 2001-2007 as “relative stability”; the page’s series nonetheless records a rise from 59.3% to 65.5% of GDP, and the OFCE estimates that nearly half of the deficit gap with the euro area had formed before the 2007-2008 crisis.
Challenged by.
Any reading of the near-cancellation of interest and growth as neutrality.
The match depends on the starting point, to within about ten points (Auclert, Philippon and Ragot), and the gap between interest and growth changed sign during the period (Clavères).
This page’s data confirm it: the interest-growth effect contributed +16.9 points before 2017, −15.6 since.
The CAE note, for its part, interprets this accounting finding as the direct result of budgetary choices (“our debt is the direct consequence of our budgetary choices”, our translation), while judging the share of crises hard to estimate; an OFCE estimate, cited by the note, puts it at about half of the rise since 2007.
Not established.
Which of spending or revenue widened the recent deficits.
The OFCE, which puts forward a more gradual adjustment path, starts the recent period at the 2017 presidential election and attributes most of the deterioration of the structural balance to an unfunded fall in compulsory levies, which declined by 2.5 points of GDP from 2017 to 2024; over that period, according to the OFCE, total spending stays stable as a share of GDP and primary spending falls slightly as a share of potential GDP (on Eurostat’s series, as a share of actual GDP, total spending changes by −0.7 points).
The French Treasury, for which consolidation “could go first” (our translation) through cutting spending, starts from 2019, the situation before the pandemic, and notes that several spending items (interest charge, social security, central and local government) contributed to the 2.7-point deterioration of the balance up to 2025, while noting that reforms durably reduced revenue.
Over the common window, the OFCE and the French Treasury converge on one fact: public spending rose less in France than in the euro area (1.8 points against 2.6 according to the OFCE, 1.6 against 2.4 according to the Treasury); Eurostat’s series find the same direction and order of magnitude (spending +1.7 points, total revenue −1.8).
They do not, however, then analyse the same object: the OFCE explains the gap between France and the euro area, which it links mainly to revenue, while the French Treasury breaks down the deterioration of the French balance over 2019-2025; and Eurostat’s total revenue is not the OFCE’s compulsory levies.
On Eurostat’s series, the diagnosis depends on both ends of the window (section “Too much spending or too little revenue?”).
The Court of Audit separates, for compulsory levies, decided measures from spontaneous growth; its reports from 2017 to 2026 feed the section “Tax cuts, or revenue that slows down?”, which reproduces its 2017-2022 breakdown and measures what tax credits change in the split between revenue and spending.
The causes, finally: the CAE note attributes the recent deterioration to the pandemic, the energy price shields and unfunded tax cuts, the French Treasury to the crises and the support measures, but none of the texts read provides a causal decomposition of the primary deficits observed from 1995 to 2025.

Summary
What to take away
Once interest and growth have almost offset each other, what remains in the Eurostat decomposition used here is primary deficits, 87% of the rise: excluding interest, the public accounts were in surplus in only 4 years out of 30.
These figures separate the terms of the rise, not the reasons for budget decisions.
This near-cancellation is an offset over time, not neutrality.
Before 2017, the implicit interest rate usually exceeded nominal growth, and the interest-growth effect added 16.9 points to the ratio; since then, nominal growth has prevailed every year up to 2024, except 2020, when GDP fell, and the effect has contributed −15.6 points.
In 2025, on accounts still subject to revision, the implicit rate moves slightly back above nominal growth (1.99% against 1.90%): the gap is too thin to say that a new period is starting.
As long as the two stay this close, the direction in which the ratio moves is set by the primary balance, give or take stock-flow adjustments; if the gap is confirmed, the ratio will rise more for the same primary balance.
Spending or revenue: the accounts describe movements, they do not designate anyone responsible, and their answer changes with the two years being compared.

Frequently asked questions
Does interest make the debt rise?
Yes: it is a real charge, which adds to the borrowing requirement.
But nominal GDP growth lowers the debt-to-GDP ratio by enlarging its denominator, and over thirty years it almost offset the accounting contribution of interest (73.9 points, against 72.6 of reduction).
This offset is made of two opposite periods: before 2017, the net effect added 16.9 points to the ratio; since then, it has taken off 15.6.
In 2025, on accounts still subject to revision, the implicit interest rate and nominal growth were almost equal (1.99% and 1.90%).
Does the debt come from too much spending or too little revenue?
These accounts do not settle it: a primary deficit is a gap, which can come from one, the other or both.
On Eurostat’s series, the diagnosis changes with the years compared: from 2017 to 2024, public revenue changes by −3.1 points of GDP and spending excluding interest by −0.9; from 2019 to 2025, spending excluding interest by +1.3 and revenue by −0.8; from 2019 to 2024, revenue by −1.8 and spending excluding interest by +1.2.
The end year matters as much as the start year.
Does inflation reduce the debt?
It can reduce the ratio when it lifts nominal GDP growth above the average interest rate paid on the debt (the implicit interest rate).
From 2021 to 2023, nominal growth — price rises included — produced an interest-growth effect of −16.8 points, while primary deficits added 11.5; this decomposition does not separate the share of prices from that of real growth.
This is not costless: it reduces the real value of nominal claims, and it raises the cost of inflation-linked bonds.
Is a primary deficit a sign of too much spending?
Not necessarily: it is a gap between spending and revenue excluding interest, which can come from rising spending, falling revenue, or a recession that does both at once.
These data measure the gap; they do not say which of its two terms should be corrected.

Where these figures come from
Eurostat, general government (S.13), national accounts ESA 2010, in national currency: Maastricht debt (gov_10dd_edpt1), interest due (gov_10a_main, D41PAY, recorded on an accrual basis), net lending or borrowing (B9), nominal GDP as published with the deficit and debt notification (gov_10dd_edpt1, B1GQ); spending (TE), revenue (TR) and balance as a share of GDP as Eurostat publishes them (gov_10a_main).
The primary balance is the general government balance plus interest (D41PAY).
Each year, the identity is applied as written above; stock-flow adjustments are the residual.
The script then compares, year by year, the debt ratio and the primary balance it computes with those Eurostat publishes as a percentage of GDP, and stops beyond a gap of 0.11 points.
The figures computed from Eurostat series all come from the same script, re-run with each release of the sources, which checks the main quantitative statements and stops if their conditions are no longer met; their wording is reviewed editorially.
Figures attributed to other studies (CAE, OFCE, French Treasury) are given as they publish them.
Indexation cost of State bonds in 2022: French Senate, report on the 2022 budget settlement bill (in French).
Section “Tax cuts, or revenue that slows down?”: new measures, spontaneous growth and nominal GDP growth transcribed from the Court of Audit’s reports on the state and outlook of public finances, using the latest vintage for each year, and checked against the archived reports; INSEE’s rate of compulsory levies net of tax credits (national accounts, base 2020, in French); Eurostat’s payable tax credits (PTC) and total revenue (gov_10a_main).
Each year, the Court’s identity, Δτ = τt−1 × (s − g) / (1 + g) + new measures / GDP, with s the spontaneous growth and g GDP growth, is checked against the rate published by INSEE; the script stops if the gap exceeds 0.3 points in any year, or if the calculation strays more than 0.15 points from the breakdown the Court publishes for 2017-2022.
The method behind this decomposition (the identity, the benchmark formed by the ratios Eurostat publishes, and the error it rejected) and its reconciliation with three public readings of the same debt (the 2014 citizens’ audit, the iFRAP Foundation, the French Court of Audit) are set out in the working paper AWP-09 — What Public Accounts Can Establish about the Displacement of the Public Debt Burden (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23145963, open-access PDF).

What is checked, and how.
Three operations should be distinguished when reading the method of these pages.
Numerical checking.
Depending on the page, figures are calculated by the script that writes the page, from published series, or taken from an archived calculation; this script checks the main numerical claims and stops if their conditions no longer hold.
The decompositions of the ratio are also compared, year by year, with the ratio and the primary balance that Eurostat publishes, with the tolerance and exclusions each page states.
Documentary checking.
Values taken from a document (Cour des comptes report, budget act, Banque de France or Agence France Trésor publication, study) are transcribed with the source the page cites; a study’s figures are reproduced as it publishes them, remain attributed to it and are kept apart from the recalculations made for these pages.
Editorial judgement.
The choice of periods, presentation thresholds, comparisons and words is the author’s.
Calculations check the consequences of these choices, not their relevance: they are reviewed, independent external reviews are reported where they are documented, and they are stated as such where they matter (a presentation threshold is not a result).

In three sentences
From end-1995 to end-2025, the French debt-to-GDP ratio went from 57.8% to 115.7%.
Of these 57.9 points, primary deficits contributed 50.5, stock-flow adjustments 6.1 and the net effect of interest and nominal growth 1.3, made of two opposite periods.
This is an accounting decomposition, not a causal attribution: it does not say why the deficits existed, and the answer to “spending or revenue” changes with the two years compared.
