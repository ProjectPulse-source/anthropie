Is the world economy decoupling from energy?
The world economy uses less and less energy for each unit of wealth it produces: 28.5% less in 2024 than in 2000.
Its energy consumption, meanwhile, rose by 58% over the same period.
Falling energy intensity slowed the rise; it did not reverse it.

Intensity falls, consumption rises
From 2000 to 2024, world GDP, measured at purchasing power parity in constant prices, rose by 121%.
The energy used to produce each unit of that GDP, energy intensity, fell by 28.5%.
And world primary energy consumption went from 405 to 639 exajoules (one exajoule is 10¹⁸ joules): 58% more.
The world economy has therefore decoupled from energy in the relative sense: its consumption grows more slowly than its output.
It has not decoupled in the absolute sense, where consumption would fall while the economy grows.
Primary energy consumed on the territory, EIA convention; effects computed with a Divisia index, which add up exactly to the change (EIA, international data).
The result in one sentence.
From 2000 to 2024, world energy consumption rose by 58%: the decomposition attributes 407 exajoules of increase to population and GDP per head, and only 172 of decrease to energy intensity.
Population or growing wealth?
It is often said that energy consumption rises because the world’s population rises.
An accounting decomposition separates the two.
Energy consumption is the product of three terms: population, GDP per head, and the energy used per unit of GDP.
From 2000 to 2024, the decomposition attributes 144 exajoules to the population effect, 263 to the GDP-per-head effect and −172 to falling intensity: 235 exajoules more in all.
GDP per head therefore weighed 1.8 times as much as demography.
That ranking does not depend on the starting year: in each of the windows tested, starting in 1990, 2000, 2010, 2015 and 2019 and ending in 2024, the GDP-per-head effect exceeds the population effect, and consumption rose.

An accounting decomposition is not an explanation.
It says how much each term weighed, not why.
It does not say that getting richer causes the rise in consumption, nor that falling intensity comes from more efficient uses: intensity also falls when the economy shifts towards services, or when a measurement convention counts electricity differently.
Nor can it be used to estimate a rebound effect.
Why the intensity effect (−172 exajoules) exceeds the fall in intensity itself
The 28.5% measures the change in intensity; the 172 exajoules, its contribution to the change in consumption.
The decomposition measures each change in logarithms, which makes the sum of the effects exact.
Intensity was multiplied by 0.715 (a fall of 28.5%), whose logarithm is −0.34.
That value applies to the period’s average consumption, 513 exajoules (a so-called logarithmic mean, between 405 and 639): hence an effect of −172 exajoules.
Set against the 405 exajoules of 2000, it is worth 42.6%, the figure in the chart: it is the same fall in intensity, measured on a wider base, not a second fall.
The European Union: absolute decoupling, on its territory
The same producer, with the same definition, gives the other case for the EU-27.
From 2000 to 2024, its primary energy consumption fell by 18%, from 66 to 54 exajoules, while its GDP rose by 43%; it peaked in 2006.
The population effect adds 3 exajoules, the GDP-per-head effect 18, and the 42.4% fall in intensity takes away 33: more than the other two combined.
The end year matters.
Stopping in 2019, before the pandemic and the 2022 surge in energy prices, European consumption falls by 6.4% from 2000, with GDP up 34%.
Absolute decoupling is already there, but more than half of the fall measured to 2024 came after 2019; these data do not say how much of it will last.
What the comparison does not say.
These figures count the energy consumed on European territory, not the energy used to make the goods Europe imports.
They cannot tell whether the fall comes from efficiency, from the decline of the most energy-intensive industries or from their relocation outside Europe.
Settling that would require an energy footprint of European demand, which we have not found published in a reusable form.
Does the result depend on the source?
A measurement convention, checked year by year.
Primary energy is not counted the same way everywhere.
In the EIA’s files, we measure every year from 1990 to 2024 the ratio between the energy counted and the electricity generated: 3,412 Btu per kilowatt-hour for hydro and wind, that is, their electrical content, with no change of convention; from 10,400 to 10,700 Btu for nuclear, counted at the heat produced by the reactor.
Solar is counted at 3,443 Btu in 2024, at a higher ratio in earlier years, when it weighed very little.
The Energy Institute, which publishes the Statistical Review of World Energy, has applied the same physical-content logic since its 2025 edition: the comparison below tests another producer, not another convention.
Another producer.
With the Energy Institute’s total energy supply (2026 edition: 592 exajoules in 2024) and World Bank GDP and population, the decomposition keeps the same ranking: world consumption up 55% from 2000 to 2024; GDP-per-head effect 247 exajoules, population 133, intensity −170.
For the European Union, the fall is 17% instead of 18%.
Eurostat, which publishes its primary energy consumption indicator only from 2005, gives the same size within a few points: from 2005 to 2024, EU consumption falls by 20% according to Eurostat, 22% according to the Energy Institute and 23% according to the EIA.
Levels differ more (50 exajoules in 2024 for Eurostat, 54 for the EIA): Eurostat’s indicator leaves out, among other things, non-energy uses of fuels.
How GDP is converted matters.
The figures on this page convert GDP at purchasing power parity.
At market exchange rates, world GDP rises by 99% from 2000 to 2024, and intensity falls by only 21%; the GDP-per-head effect drops to 210 exajoules and the intensity effect to −120, while the population effect does not change.
Under both conversions, in every window, consumption rises, intensity falls and GDP per head weighs more than population.
How much intensity falls, however, depends on the conversion: at market exchange rates, from 2000 to 2024, it takes away less than population adds.
Confronting the research
Paul Brockway and four co-authors, researchers in energy economics and engineering (universities of Leeds, Sussex, Massachusetts, SOAS and Calvin, IFP Energies nouvelles), reviewed in 2021 thirty-three studies that estimate the rebound effect for a whole economy, twenty-one based on general equilibrium models and twelve on other methods.
They conclude that these effects may erode more than half of the energy savings expected from efficiency, and that global energy scenarios, which take too little account of them, may underestimate future demand.
On the observation, our figures agree with theirs: from 1971 to 2018, they write, the world experienced no absolute decoupling, only national cases; from 2000 to 2024, the EIA series shows the same.
On the explanation, this page does not test them.
The same authors stress that an improvement in efficiency measured at one level does not necessarily bring an improvement measured at another: an aggregate energy intensity does not measure efficiency, and no rebound rate can be derived from a decomposition like this one.
What these data do not say
A decomposition, not a cause.
The three effects add up exactly to the change in consumption; they attribute no part of it to a cause.
Intensity is not efficiency.
Energy per unit of GDP also falls when the make-up of the economy changes, or when the measurement convention changes.
A measurement convention.
The EIA and the Energy Institute count hydro and wind electricity at its electrical content.
Counted as the fossil fuel it replaces (the so-called substitution method), its growth would weigh more, and intensity would fall less; that variant is not computed here.
Territory, not footprint.
Energy embodied in imports is not counted: this is the decisive limit of the comparison with the European Union.
GDP at purchasing power parity.
It depends on a base year and on revisions; the EIA’s constant prices are checked against the World Bank at each update, and the variant at market exchange rates is given above.
One decomposition method.
The effects are those of the logarithmic mean Divisia index; no other decomposition method has been tried.
A world aggregate.
It adds up very different regional trajectories, which this page does not detail.
A reading by the author, not tested here.
The notion of the rebound effect refers to the renewed consumption that erodes an efficiency gain.
In the working paper AWP-04, the economist Stéphane Lalut places it among the transfer mechanisms of the energy transition.
The figures on this page describe relative decoupling; they do not measure a rebound and do not demonstrate that reading.
