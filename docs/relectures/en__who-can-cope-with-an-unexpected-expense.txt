Who can cope with an unexpected expense?
Is being unable to face an unexpected expense a matter of poverty?
Only in part.
In France, in 2025, 65.9% of people below the poverty threshold are in this situation, and 21.6% above it.
But above the threshold, situations differ sharply by household composition: 46.9% of people in single-parent households could not face an unexpected expense, against 18.6% of people in two-adult households with one child.

Who could not face an unexpected expense?
In France, in 2025, 28.8% of people live in a household that says it could not meet an unexpected expense of €1,000 from its own resources: nearly three in ten.
The gap between people on either side of the poverty threshold is very marked:
below the poverty threshold above the threshold
could not face the expense, in 2025 about two thirds 65.9% about one in five 21.6%
But being above or below the threshold is not the whole story.
Above the threshold, where an income-based classification says “not poor”, situations differ sharply by household composition.
Share of people (not households), as reported by their household; poverty threshold: 60% of national median equivalised disposable income (Eurostat, EU-SILC).
The result in one sentence.
In France, in 2025, above the poverty threshold, 46.9% of people in single-parent households (one adult with one or more children) could not face an unexpected expense of €1,000, against 18.6% of people in two-adult households with one child, a gap of 28.3 points.
What the comparison can — and cannot — tell us
Being above the poverty threshold does not guarantee being able to absorb an unexpected expense.
Among people above that threshold in France in 2025, 46.9% of those in a household of one adult with one or more children could not, according to their household, face an expense of €1,000, against 18.6% of those in two-adult households with one child.
The gap is 28.3 points.
It describes two groups: it does not measure what would happen to a person if their household went from two adults to one.
These groups may also differ in their exact income above the threshold, the number and age of children, employment, committed expenses and the benefits they receive.
The data published here cannot tell these explanations apart.
Above the threshold, the series allows other comparisons:
With children, the gap between households of one adult with one or more children and two-adult households with one child has stayed between 20 and 30 points every year since 2015.
Without children, the gap between one- and two-adult households is only 9.4 points in 2025 (between 9 and 13 points each year over the same period).
Compared with two-adult households with two children, the gap for households of one adult with one or more children is still 29.3 points in 2025.
The comparison does not, however, measure the effect of the number of children: the Eurostat category this page calls a single-parent household covers any household of one adult with one or more dependent children (under 18, or aged 18 to 24 if economically inactive and living with a parent), and the two-adult category any household of two adults, whether or not they are a couple.
Above the threshold, the gap between one- and two-adult households is thus two to three times as wide with children as without.
It has persisted every year since 2015, but it does not isolate the effect of household composition itself: the number and age of children, employment, committed expenses and the benefits received may also differ between these households.
Below the threshold, the gap between single-parent households and two-adult households with one child is smaller (5.1 points in 2025): in both, a large majority of people could not face an unexpected expense.
Being above or below the threshold is therefore not enough to explain these gaps.
What the comparison does not settle.
Above the threshold, single-parent households might on average sit closer to the threshold than two-adult households with one child.
The Eurostat dataset does not show how far each household’s income lies above the threshold, so it cannot tell how much of the gap goes with household composition and how much with that distance.
The same finding across the European Union
In the EU-27, 29.2% of people could not face an unexpected expense in 2025.
Above the poverty threshold, the gap between households of one adult with one or more children and two-adult households with one child is 22.8 points (between 19 and 25 points every year since 2015), against 10.1 points between one- and two-adult households without children (between 9 and 11 points).
The finding is not specific to France.
What these data do not say
A statement, not a bank balance.
The question asks whether the household could pay an unexpected expense without outside help or new borrowing; it measures neither savings nor available assets.
An amount below the one set by the common rule.
In France, the 2025 survey asks about an expense of €1,000 (Insee).
Eurostat’s guidelines set this amount, country by country, at one twelfth of the poverty threshold taken from the survey two years earlier, rounded to within 5%: about €1,209 for France in 2025, based on the 2023 threshold.
The French question therefore asks about a smaller sum, which matters when the French share is compared with that of other countries.
It does not bias comparisons between French households in a given year, since they all answer the same question; but the size of the gaps may depend on the amount asked, which may have changed from one year to the next.
People, not households.
Shares are computed over people: a child in a household that could not face the expense is counted among the people concerned.
The poverty threshold is relative.
It is 60% of national median equivalised disposable income, after social transfers; it accounts for household size and composition, not for the reserve the household holds.
An association, not a cause.
The chart shows that household composition and inability to face an unexpected expense go together, not that one produces the other; how far incomes lie from the threshold is not taken into account.
Margin of error not published.
Eurostat publishes no confidence intervals for these subgroups; none of the values used carries Eurostat’s “low reliability” flag, but EU values before 2019 are flagged as estimated.
The absence of that flag does not make a gap of a few tenths of a point significant: the page comments only on gaps of several points, without being able to say, for lack of published intervals, how large a gap between these small groups must be to be reliable.
Breaks in series.
Eurostat flags breaks in the French series in 2020 and 2022: multi-year ranges should be read with caution.
The main finding compares household types in the same year, and these breaks do not affect it.
The author’s hypothesis, not tested here.
In the working paper AWP-08, the economist Stéphane Lalut proposes reading individual fallback capacity as one of the three components of what he calls social reversibility: what a person can afford to fail at without durably closing off their future.
The indicator on this page, based on what the household reports, captures only the financial side of that capacity; it says nothing about the cost of a failure or the possibility of trying again.
Further reading: La Société du premier coup (in French) shows, through concrete cases, what a failure costs depending on the social position of the person who suffers it, and why some cannot afford to try.
Frequently asked questions
What share of people in France could not face an unexpected expense?
In 2025, 28.8% of people living in France belong to a household that says it could not face, from its own resources, an unexpected expense of €1,000 (Eurostat, EU-SILC survey).
The share is 65.9% below the poverty threshold and 21.6% above it.
Are people in single-parent households more often unable to face an unexpected expense, even when they are not poor?
Yes, as an association, not a cause; the gap is wider above the poverty threshold than below it.
In France, in 2025, among people above the threshold, 46.9% of those in single-parent households (one adult with one or more children) could not face an unexpected expense of €1,000, against 18.6% of those in two-adult households with one child, a gap of 28.3 points.
Without children, the gap between one- and two-adult households is only 9.4 points.
The series does not say whether the gap comes from household composition, from incomes closer to the threshold or from other differences between these households (number and age of children, employment, committed expenses, benefits received).
Where do these figures come from?
From the EU Statistics on Income and Living Conditions survey (EU-SILC), Eurostat dataset ilc_mdes04 (series since 2004 for France and 2010 for the EU-27, up to 2025), retrieved by a script that stops if a figure no longer meets the conditions on which the page’s main sentences depend.
Only the amount asked about in the 2025 survey, €1,000 in France, is transcribed from an Insee publication.
