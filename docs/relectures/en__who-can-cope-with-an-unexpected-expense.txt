Who can cope with an unexpected expense?
Is being unable to face an unexpected expense a matter of poverty?
Only in part.
In France, in 2025, 65.9% of people below the poverty threshold are in this situation, and 21.6% above it.
But above the threshold, household composition changes everything: 46.9% of single parents could not face an unexpected expense, against 18.6% of couples with one child.

Who could not face an unexpected expense?
In France, in 2025, 28.8% of people live in a household that, by its own account, could not face an unexpected expense: nearly three in ten.
Income weighs heavily.
Below the poverty threshold the share reaches 65.9%, about two thirds; above it, 21.6%, about one in five.
But income is not the whole story.
Above the threshold, where an income-based classification says “not poor”, household composition sharply separates situations.
Share of people, self-reported; poverty threshold: 60% of median equivalised income (Eurostat, EU-SILC).
The result in one sentence.
In France, in 2025, above the poverty threshold, 46.9% of single parents could not face an unexpected expense, against 18.6% of couples with one child: 2.5 times as high.
Two explanations, one test
One might think that everything turns on income: being above or below the poverty threshold.
If so, two households on the same side of the threshold should show similar shares.
The test compares households that differ first of all in one thing: one adult more or less.
With one child, moving from two adults to one adds 28.3 points above the threshold in 2025, and between 21 and 30 points every year since 2015.
Without children, the same move adds only 9.4 points (between 9 and 13 points over the same period).
The number of children is not enough to explain the gap.
The statistics count as a “single parent” any single adult with one child or more; against couples with two children, the gap remains 29.3 points.
The gap linked to being alone is thus two to three times larger when there are children, and it is clearest above the poverty threshold.
Below it, the gap narrows (5.1 points in 2025): single parents and couples with one child are both exposed in a large majority.
Being above or below the threshold is therefore not enough to explain these gaps.
What the test does not settle.
A third explanation remains possible: above the threshold, single parents might on average sit closer to the threshold than couples with one child.
The Eurostat dataset does not detail position within the income band, so it cannot separate what household composition weighs from what that position weighs.
The same finding across the European Union
In the EU-27, 29.2% of people could not face an unexpected expense in 2025.
Above the poverty threshold, moving from two adults to one adds 22.8 points with one child (between 20 and 24 points since 2015), against 10.1 points without children (between 10 and 11).
The finding is not specific to France.
What these data do not say
A statement, not a bank balance.
The question asks whether the household could cover an unexpected expense of an amount set by the survey for each country; it measures neither savings nor available assets.
People, not households.
Shares are computed over people: a child in an exposed household counts as exposed.
The poverty threshold is relative.
It is 60% of median equivalised income; it accounts for household size, not for the reserve the household holds.
An association, not a cause.
The chart shows that household composition and vulnerability to a shock go together, not that one produces the other; position within the income band is not controlled.
Breaks in series.
Eurostat flags breaks in France in 2020 and 2022: multi-year ranges should be read with caution.
The main finding compares households in the same year, and the break does not affect it.
A hypothesis of the author, not tested here.
In the working paper AWP-08, the economist Stéphane Lalut proposes reading financial fallback capacity as one of the three components of what the author calls social reversibility: what a person can afford to fail without durably closing their future.
The indicator on this page measures only part of it; it says nothing about the cost of a failure or the possibility of trying again.
To go further: La Société du premier coup (in French) tells, through concrete cases, what a failure costs depending on the social position of the person who suffers it, and why some cannot afford to try.
