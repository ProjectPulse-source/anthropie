Can the rules of the public debt game be changed?
Yes, but with no automatic effect: depending on its mechanism and conditions, the same operation can reduce debt, shift burdens or risks onto others, or worsen it.
With unchanged policy, the European Commission projects French public debt at 126.5% of GDP in 2030 and 140.8% in 2035; changing the rules of the game alters that path only through the budget balance, interest rates, growth or the stock of debt, and the nine ways of doing so examined here do not involve the same decision-makers or the same agreements.

Three questions to examine each operation.
A debt can disappear from a balance sheet while the obligations, costs or risks attached to it remain; a reform can also produce a real gain.
To tell the two apart, each operation examined here goes through the same questions.
What effect?
Reducing the debt ratio, easing interest payments or transferring an obligation do not produce the same result.
Who decides?
Depending on the operation: the Government and Parliament, under European surveillance, or the agreement of the Council of the Union, the central bank, each Member State or creditors.
Who pays?
The taxpayers targeted, holders of securities and their savers, other States or later years.

Where does the current path lead?
Three kinds of figures circulate about the future of debt, and they do not say the same thing.
The European Commission projects debt with unchanged policy: with no new measure, what the ratio would become with only the decisions already taken.
It is neither a forecast nor the most likely scenario, as the Commission itself writes.
The Commission is not an outside observer: it oversees compliance with the European fiscal rules.
Its baseline serves here as a common yardstick because it is published, dated and reproducible, not on any claim to neutrality.
The Government publishes commitments, in October 2024 in its medium-term fiscal-structural plan, and forecasts, in 2026 in the debt report annexed to the 2027 draft budget: they assume measures not all of which have been passed.
This page adds the third kind, sensitivities: what a single parameter would change, on the Commission’s baseline.
French public debt, % of GDP: distribution of the paths simulated by the European Commission (10th to 90th percentiles), unchanged-policy path, 2027 draft budget forecast and October 2024 commitment.
These paths do not start from the same point in 2025 and do not assume the same policies.
The result in one sentence.
With unchanged policy, the Commission projects debt at 126.5% of GDP in 2030, against 116.3% in 2025, and puts at 95% the probability that debt in 2030 exceeds its 2025 level.
Reading the fan.
The Government’s forecast, 120.9%, sits in the lower half of this fan, around the 20th percentile: about 20% of the simulated paths end lower.
The two exercises, however, rest on different policies, starting points and economic assumptions: this rank measures neither the specific effect of the Government’s decisions nor their probability of success.
If the economy goes through growth, interest-rate and budget swings similar to those France has already experienced, debt in 2030 would lie, in 80% of the Commission’s simulations, between 118.1% and 136.2% of GDP, a gap of 18.1 points: these are the chart’s bands, which the Commission calculates up to 2030.
These probabilities come from the Commission’s model and assume no new measures.
They do not measure the probability that a government keeps its commitments, and this page attributes none to a political choice.
Beyond 2030 the Commission no longer publishes a fan: its unchanged-policy projection reaches 140.8% in 2035.
The 2024 commitment, the 2026 forecast.
In October 2024 the Government committed to debt of 116.5% of GDP in 2027; in 2026 it forecasts 121.7%, 5.2 points more, two years after the commitment.
Where these paths sit within the Commission’s fan
Placed within the Commission’s fan, the 2024 commitment for 2029, 115.8%, falls below the 10th percentile in level.
But it started lower: the three paths do not start from the same point in 2025 (116.3% for the Commission, 115.7% in the 2027 draft budget, 114.7% in the 2024 plan).
Measured as a change since 2025, the same commitment sits around the 12th percentile, and the 2027 draft budget’s forecast for 2030 around the 24th.
A path’s rank in the fan therefore also depends on its starting point.
What the balance, interest rates and growth change
A debt path moves only through four channels: the primary balance, that is revenue minus spending excluding interest, the interest rate paid on the debt, nominal GDP growth, and operations on the stock of debt itself.
Any reform, including a change of rule, acts on the path only through them.
The chart applies a single change at a time to the Commission’s baseline.
Gap in public debt in 2035 relative to the European Commission’s baseline, in points of GDP, one parameter changed at a time (author’s calculations on the Debt Sustainability Monitor 2025 baseline).
The balance.
A primary balance 1 percentage point of GDP higher every year from 2028 to 2035 would lower debt by 3.0 points in 2030 and by 8.2 points in 2035.
It is an accounting calculation: it assumes the adjustment neither slows growth nor changes interest rates, which it would partly do.
One point of GDP of borrowed investment every year, with no effect on growth, would do the opposite: 8.2 points more.
Interest rates.
Market rates 0.2 percentage point (twenty basis points) lower on all new public borrowing would lower debt by 1.0 point in 2035 and the interest bill by about €8.1 billion that year; ten basis points lower, by 0.5 points.
A rise of one point would raise it by 5.5 points, and the interest bill by about €44.6 billion.
The effect is slow: a market rate first affects only short-term debt and the securities issued during the year, then the rest as it is rolled over.
Growth.
Half a point of extra nominal growth every year would lower debt by 5.7 points in 2035; half a point less would raise it by 5.9 points.
Growth is not a decision: it is an assumption, which modulates all the others.
It works together with the interest rate: by the simple arithmetic of debt, with a zero primary balance and no stock-flow adjustments, the ratio rises when the average interest rate on the debt exceeds nominal growth and falls in the opposite case; a primary deficit or stock-flow adjustments can nevertheless reverse that result.
Technical progress enters growth as one possible driver; this page assigns it no separately quantified effect.
These bars are not to be compared as equivalent efforts: a point of primary balance is a budgetary effort to be renewed every year, whereas an interest rate or growth cannot be decreed.
They give the order of magnitude of each channel, not what a policy would cost or yield.
How these sensitivities are calculated, and what they were tested against
The calculation follows the debt dynamics described by the Commission in the annex to its Debt Sustainability Monitor: short-term debt follows the market rate within the year, long-term debt only as it is rolled over; a balance shock passes through a fiscal multiplier and a cyclical component, except in the accounting calculations, where both are zero.
Before writing the page, the calculation was checked against two variants published by the Commission (a rise in the gap between interest and growth, a financial crisis): it reproduces them within tolerances written in advance, and a deliberate error in the pass-through of rates makes it fail.
It was also checked against the sensitivity published by the Agence France Trésor for a one-point rise in all rates: €8.1 billion of interest in the second year, €19.2 billion in the fifth, which the calculation finds within a few per cent; the first year (€3.4 billion) it does not, because of the issuance calendar: rate effects are therefore read only from the second year.
No outside benchmark exists for a lasting balance shock: hence the accounting calculation.
Changing the rules: who decides, who pays?
Proposals in the debate often promise to ease the debt other than through fiscal adjustment.
The nine operations below are examined one by one with the same grid: what they change, their effect when it can be calculated, who decides them, and the provisional outcome (a constraint reduced, shifted onto others, aggravated, or dependent on named conditions).
Each line carries the status of what it states: a text read, a calculation, a result established elsewhere on this site, an inference, or what is not established.

A golden rule for investment
Provisional outcome · depends on conditions Depends on the return on investment and on its financing: output, fiscal return and debt are three distinct effects.
What changes inferred
How investment is treated by the rules; debt rises if it is borrowed.
Effect calculation
One point of GDP of borrowed investment every year from 2028 to 2035, with no effect on growth, raises debt by 8.2 points in 2035.
The accounting calculation does not measure the economic effects of investment: published results diverge with its return, its financing and the assumptions made.
Who decides text read
Depending on the variant: excluding some spending from net expenditure monitoring requires amending the regulation (ordinary legislative procedure); flexibility for investment is possible depending on its terms; excluding investment from the reference deficit touches Protocol No 12, which only the Council acting unanimously can replace (Art.
126(14) TFEU).
Published estimates, law, who gains and who pays, robustness, sources
Published estimates text read
IMF staff (2014, seventeen advanced economies, 1985-2013) estimate that one point of public investment raises output by 0.4% in the same year and by 1.5% after four years, the fall in the debt ratio (0.9 points) being significant only in the short run, under conditions; Commission staff (2024, model calibrated on Germany) find that an uncompensated rise in investment lastingly raises the ratio.
Conditional results, not directly transferable to France in 2028-2035.
Applicable law text read
Protocol No 12 defines the deficit as “net borrowing” and investment as “gross fixed capital formation”; the Treaty already asks the Commission to examine whether the deficit exceeds investment (Art.
126(3)), as a criterion of its report, not as an exemption.
Who gains, who pays inferred
Towards later years if the fiscal return is below the cost of financing; a positive social return is not necessarily a sufficient fiscal return.
Robustness documented mechanism
Higher debt, more exposed to rates, as long as the return is not secured.

Existing commitments and the currency
7
Converting or cancelling bonds held by the Eurosystem
Provisional outcome · depends on conditions Net saving not secured; effects depend on the scheme and on monetary conditions.
What changes inferred
It depends on the operation.
Converting into zero-coupon perpetual bonds that keep their face value does not necessarily reduce debt as defined by the Treaty; a cancellation that extinguishes the claim must be examined separately, with its accounting counterpart and its effect on the deficit.
Effect established on this site
The Banque de France held €546 billion of French government securities at 31 December 2025.
The gross accounting effect and the net saving are assessed separately: the net saving depends on the gap between the interest paid on bank reserves and the State’s refinancing cost, and is not secured.
Who decides text read
The competent Eurosystem authorities and, depending on the operation, the debtor State; neither the ECB nor a national central bank may “seek or take instructions” from governments (Art.
130 TFEU): under the current Treaties, the State cannot impose the operation.

Cross-cutting variable: growth.
A calculation assumption, not a decision, whose effect is quantified above with the other levers.
It modulates every row: the return on an investment, the cost of a loan and the yield of a tax all depend on it.
Technology enters as a possible driver, with no separately quantified effect.

Five distinctions for understanding these operations.
The four channels say how the effect on debt travels; these distinctions say what must be kept apart to judge each operation.

The same reform can reduce measured debt, shift burdens, change risks or produce opposite effects, depending on how it is implemented.
Of the nine operations, none can be classified once and for all: most depend on a condition the grid names, the return on an investment, the actual cost of joint borrowing, the reaction of creditors or taxpayers.
Changing the rules of debt does not amount to choosing between paying and not paying: each operation changes a set of rights, obligations, risks and capacities.
Changing the rules can also change who decides.
Some operations lie mainly with French institutions, under European surveillance; others require the agreement of the Council of the Union, the central bank, each Member State or creditors.
The possibility of acting depends as much on these agreements as on the economic result sought.
Each card says who bears the cost (targeted taxpayers, bondholders and their savers, other States, later years) under “Who gains, who pays”, in its fold-out.
The economic size of a reform says nothing about its legal difficulty.
Excluding some spending from European monitoring of net expenditure requires amending a regulation, by qualified majority; joint borrowing, even capped, requires unanimity and the approval of each State; changing the definition of the reference deficit, unanimity in the Council.
European law distinguishes three things the debate conflates: flexibility in fiscal paths (extending the adjustment, treating investment), European borrowing (lending to a State or spending for the Union), and taking over another State’s commitments, which the Treaties strictly limit.
These are not three variants of the same reform.
What these projections do not say
Projection, forecast, sensitivity.
The Commission’s baseline is a conditional unchanged-policy projection, not a forecast of the most likely outcome.
The Government’s paths are forecasts, resting on economic assumptions and on measures some of which have yet to be enacted.
The sensitivities change one parameter at a time.
None of these figures is certain.
One lever at a time.
The sensitivities and the matrix take operations one by one; no combination is calculated, whereas joint borrowing and a golden rule, or a restructuring and subsequent interest rates, interact.
A ratio does not measure welfare.
Spending can raise the ratio and improve public assets or avoid a future cost; a sale can lower it without making anyone richer.
A gap in points of GDP is not an amount paid.
Feasible is not acceptable.
The matrix examines the known legal conditions, the agreements required and what remains to be established; it does not say what a majority would accept.
What remains to be read.
The statistical classification of an interest-free perpetual security, the Eurosystem’s texts on a conversion, the creation orders of three index-linked bond lines, Article 65 of the Treaty on exceptions to the free movement of capital and the Conseil d’État’s opinion on a differential wealth tax (2025) have not been read: the cards concerned say so.
The author’s hypothesis, separate from the findings.
The findings above stand without it.
The author reads them in the light of anthropy, which the author defines as follows:
Anthropy is the hypothesis that social systems displace disorder rather than resolve it.
Applied to debt, this hypothesis asks whether a transformation of the debt system can reduce the constraint without transferring it elsewhere: to other taxpayers, other creditors, other States or other years.
The matrix does not demonstrate it; it provides, operation by operation, the elements that would allow it to be tested.
Summary
Key points
With unchanged policy, the European Commission projects French public debt at 126.5% of GDP in 2030 and 140.8% in 2035; the Government’s forecast for 2030, 120.9%, assumes new measures.
A path changes only through the primary balance, interest rates, growth or the stock.
On the Commission’s baseline, a primary balance one percentage point of GDP higher every year from 2028 to 2035 would lower the debt ratio by 8.2 points in 2035; nominal growth half a point higher every year, by 5.7 points; market rates twenty basis points lower, by 1.0 point.
These are conditional sensitivities, not the guaranteed effects of a policy.
Changing the rules of the game spares none of these channels: depending on the operation, it can change the powers and agreements required, and reduce debt, shift its burden or worsen it.
The matrix of nine operations says so for each, with the status of every statement.
Frequently asked questions
What will French public debt be in 2030?
Nobody knows: the available figures are conditional projections.
With unchanged policy, the European Commission projects 126.5% of GDP in 2030; under shocks comparable to past ones, in 80% of the Commission’s simulations debt would be between 118.1% and 136.2%.
The Government forecasts 120.9% in the 2027 draft budget, a path that assumes new measures.
Would cancelling the debt held by the Banque de France reduce debt?
A cancellation that extinguishes the claim would lower measured debt; converting into interest-free perpetual bonds that keep their face value would not necessarily do so.
In both cases the net saving is not secured: the cost of bank reserves, remunerated by the central bank, remains and changes hands.
And, under the current Treaties, the State cannot impose the operation: neither the ECB nor the Banque de France may take instructions from a government.
Would a restructuring of French debt be legal?
Government securities of more than one year created since 2013 carry collective action clauses: the State can change their terms with qualified majorities of holders, counted in principal; for a substantial change to a single line, three quarters of the principal present or represented, provided that two thirds of the outstanding principal are present or represented, with other rules across several lines (Art.
D.
213-25-12 and D.
213-25-13 of the Monetary and Financial Code).
According to our reconstruction from the Agence France Trésor’s line sheets, 86.3% of the non-indexed nominal outstanding of bonds belongs to these lines.
The clauses make a change possible; they guarantee neither creditors’ consent nor the absence of higher interest rates afterwards.

Public debt in 2030: several futures, under which assumptions?
Where the current path leads according to the European Commission, with unchanged policy and under historical shocks, and where the paths announced by the Government in 2024 and 2026 stand.
Reading caveat.
Beyond 2030, outside the fan: the Commission’s unchanged-policy projection for 2035 is 140.8% of GDP, with no new measures after 2026.
The bands are conditional projections based on historical economic shocks; they do not predict future decisions or their consequences.
Bands, from lightest to darkest: 10th to 90th, 20th to 80th and 40th to 60th percentiles of the paths simulated by the Commission (8, 6 and 2 in 10).
The three paths rest on different assumptions and starting points.
Starting points in 2025: Commission 116.3% (February 2026 release), 2027 draft budget 115.7% (October 2026), 2024 commitment 114.7% (October 2024); the commitment gives no figure for 2026.
Sources: European Commission, Debt Sustainability Monitor 2025 (France country fiche); French government, medium-term fiscal-structural plan (October 2024) and debt report annexed to the 2027 draft budget.

Public debt in 2035: how far the primary balance, interest rates and growth move it
What a primary balance one point of GDP higher, a few tenths of a point of interest rates or half a point of growth would each change in 2035, on the Commission’s baseline.
Reading caveat.
Sensitivities: one parameter changed at a time, the others unchanged.
These are not forecasts, full policy assessments or a cost-benefit ranking.
Author’s calculations based on the European Commission’s baseline (Debt Sustainability Monitor 2025, France country fiche), engine tested against the Commission and against the sensitivity published by the French Treasury (AFT).

In three sentences
With unchanged policy, the European Commission projects French public debt at 126.5% of GDP in 2030 and 140.8% in 2035; the Government’s forecast for 2030 (120.9%) sits in the lower half of the fan of its projections.
A path changes only through the primary balance, interest rates, growth or the stock; depending on the operation, changing the rules of the game can change the powers and agreements required, and reduce debt, shift its burden or worsen it.
The Commission’s baseline is an unchanged-policy projection, the Government’s path a forecast that assumes new measures, the levers sensitivities: none of these figures is certain.
